When the site went live I said the part worth looking at was the page setting out what Meridian will not do, and a few people have come back on one line in particular, which is what happens when the evidence is simply not good enough to support an answer.

Most analysis fills that gap rather than naming it. A figure gets rounded into certainty, an assumption quietly acquires the grammar of a fact, and a sentence that should read "we do not know" ends up reading as though somebody checked. It is rarely dishonest, it is just the natural pull of a document that is expected to conclude.

Meridian is built to stop instead, and three things make it stop. The grounding rule means no figure, ruling or valuation appears in a brief unless it has been retrieved from a primary source, not remembered and not inferred from something adjacent, and where it cannot be retrieved the absence is stated rather than smoothed over. Confidence tagging means every claim carries its status as verified, reported or unverified, so a reader can see which parts of a case are solid, which are somebody else's word and which are still moving. And failing closed means that near a legal, financial or regulatory line the work names the boundary and stops rather than guessing past it, because anything binding belongs with a qualified professional.

The practical consequence is that a brief will sometimes come back saying the question cannot yet be answered, along with precisely what would have to be found before it could be. That surprises people, and nobody has yet objected, because the alternative is an answer they might act on.

All of this is published rather than promised, which means it can be held against the work rather than taken on trust.

The engine reads everything and misses nothing, and it is also the part most capable of producing a confident sentence with nothing underneath it, which is exactly why the discipline exists. The judgment at the end is mine, and so is the name on it.
