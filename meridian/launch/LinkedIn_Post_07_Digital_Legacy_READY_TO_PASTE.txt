Kate Durrant asked a question in Irish Country Living at the weekend that I have not been able to put down. Who owns your life after you die, and who gets to tell your story when you are not there to object.

She meant it as a human question. It is also a succession question, and it is the part of an estate almost nobody plans for.

The awkward bit is this. Data protection law does not apply to the dead. The GDPR says so in terms, and the Irish Act defines personal data as relating to a living individual. So the protection most people assume they have simply stops.

What is left is patchy. Copyright in a photograph belongs to whoever pressed the shutter, not to the person in the picture. Accounts are not transferable, so what becomes of yours is decided by each platform's own settings rather than by your will, and most people have never opened them.

Meanwhile a will that deals carefully with the farm, the house and the shares will often say nothing about the thousand photographs, the voice notes and the video.

I have written a lot this year about succession in family businesses. The trouble always comes from the thing nobody wrote down. This is that again, in a new form.

Three questions worth putting to your solicitor.

Who has authority over your accounts and your image after you die, and is it written down anywhere.

What would you permit, and what would you refuse. A recreated voice at a memorial is a comfort to one family and a horror to another.

Does anyone know where the material actually is, and how to get into it.

None of this needs new law. It needs the conversation had while everyone is well and still able to change their minds.

Sources: GDPR Recital 27. Data Protection Act 2018. Copyright and Related Rights Act 2000, section 21. The Protection of Voice and Image Bill 2025 is before the Oireachtas and is not yet law.
