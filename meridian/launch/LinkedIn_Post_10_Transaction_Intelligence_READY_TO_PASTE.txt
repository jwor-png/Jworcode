The Decision Brief answers one question and the Executive Intelligence Review takes a whole situation apart. This is the third, and it is for the moment a business is about to commit to something it cannot easily undo.

The term sheet is where a transaction is actually decided, and everything after it is drafting. By then the leverage has moved, because whoever asks to reopen a point looks as though they are moving the goalposts. So the work is done before signature and structured the way the other side's adviser will come at it, covering the valuation and what happens to your position in the stages that follow, the instrument with the alternatives and why each was rejected, a term by term position on what to concede and what to hold, and a read on what the counterparty will push for and how to answer it.

Three things come up again and again. The full cost of completing is almost always understated, because advisers on both sides, diligence across several disciplines, tax structuring and the treatment of the proceeds all land on whoever did not ask early enough. The same money can be worth materially different amounts depending on how it is structured, and that is usually settled by one clause agreed weeks before anyone turns their mind to tax. And protections drafted to bind one party are drafted broadly, so reading them against everything else you own is basic hygiene rather than paranoia, and it is the check most often skipped.

None of this replaces your solicitor or your accountant. It makes sure you walk into the room already knowing your position, so the advice you are paying for is spent on what only a professional can do.

The engine does the reading, the modelling and the pattern matching across every domain at once. The judgment about what to accept and what to walk away from is human, and it belongs to whoever puts their name on the signature page.
