There was a letter in the Dear Miriam column in Irish Country Living this week from someone in their late twenties working in the family business. What began as a temporary arrangement has quietly become permanent, the work is all consuming, and when they try to raise it, it lands as a lack of commitment rather than a natural wish for independence. They signed the letter "feeling torn".

Miriam's answer was kind and right on its own terms, because it is about communication and about being allowed to want your own life. But read the letter again as a business document and something else comes off the page. Every difficulty described in it is structural.

Nobody ever wrote down what the arrangement was, or defined the role, or set a date when temporary would be reviewed. Nobody said whether this person is an employee, a partner or an owner in waiting, or what happens to the business if they walk away. Because none of that was settled, it now has to be carried emotionally instead, and it is being carried by the youngest person in the room.

That is not a communication problem. It is a business with no succession plan, and the cost is being paid in somebody's twenties.

I have seen the identical pattern in farming, retail and wholesale, hospitality, construction and professional practice. The details change and the shape never does. A capable younger person is drawn in on an understanding rather than an agreement, it is never written down because writing it down would feel like mistrust, and years later nobody can say what was promised or by whom.

Succession is not a document you sign at the end. It is a series of decisions taken while everyone is still well, still talking and still able to change their minds. Who owns what and by when. What the role actually is. How the person leaving is provided for. How those not taking it on are treated fairly. What tax and legal structure do to all of that, because they will shape it whether anyone plans for it or not. And what happens if somebody dies, falls ill or simply wants out.

Those questions cross every domain at once, which is exactly why they get postponed. This is the work I care most about. Meridian sets out the position, the options and the consequences so a family can actually decide, and the binding documents are then drawn by their own solicitor and their own accountant, which is where that work belongs.

The conversation is difficult in every family I have ever seen have it. It is considerably more difficult ten years from now, and it is hardest of all when it is forced by a funeral.

If you are the one feeling torn, you are not being disloyal. You are being asked to hold something together that should have been written down years ago.
