Shane Phelan reported an experiment in the Irish Independent at the weekend that is worth the attention of anyone relying on AI for serious work, whether or not they have any interest in law.

At the Kilkenny Law Festival, four of the leading models were handed the pleadings and transcripts of a fictional extradition challenge and asked to deliver a ruling. Traps were planted in the papers, including citations attached to propositions the cases did not support, a statutory ground that had been repealed, and a reference to the wrong article of the European Convention.

The results differed and every one of them was flawed. One model refused surrender and repeated every error it had been given without checking any of it. Two ordered surrender, one citing only real authorities and catching the repealed provision, the other citing a case that could not be traced. The fourth adjourned to seek more information, which was arguably the soundest instinct in the room, and then invented a citation and treated the repealed law as though it were live.

That is the part people will quote. It is not the part that matters.

Ms Justice Marie Baker, who sat on the Supreme Court, said the judgments had a certain coherence until she stepped back and thought, in her words, what are we doing here. The challenge would never have been brought as a judicial review in the first place, and not one of the four noticed that the alleged offence, if there was one, had been committed in Ireland rather than abroad. Her verdict was that the rulings looked very polished, but none of them was right and not one picked up the big questions.

The failure was not a failure of knowledge. Every model answered the question it was given. Not one asked whether it was the right question.

I would draw two things from it. The first is that fabrication and false grounding are not edge cases to be designed out later, which is why nothing should reach a decision-maker unless it has been retrieved from a primary source rather than recalled. The second is harder. Verification catches a wrong citation. It does not catch a well-researched answer to the wrong question, and that still requires somebody who knows the territory to stop and ask what we are actually doing here.

Worth adding that the model which behaved best on process still got its sources wrong. Good instinct and poor grounding in the same document is the whole argument for a floor beneath the work rather than faith in a better model arriving.
