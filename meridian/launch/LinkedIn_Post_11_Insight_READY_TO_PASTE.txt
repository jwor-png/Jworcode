It is one of those lines that sounds obviously true until you test it against a real boardroom, and then it falls apart. Almost every organisation I have worked with already had more insight than it could use. Dashboards, reports, analyses, a data function producing good work that nobody disagreed with. What was missing was never the insight, it was somebody willing to say what it meant and carry the consequences of being wrong.

Insight is an input. It tells you what is happening and sometimes why. It does not tell you whether to buy the business, take the money, hand the farm to one child rather than another, or walk away from a deal that looks good on every metric you have. Those are judgments, and adding another dashboard has never once produced one.

The gap between knowing more and deciding better is filled by three things, none of which is data. Somebody has to weigh the evidence across every domain that could change the answer rather than the one the question arrived in. Somebody has to argue against the conclusion before it leaves, properly and at full strength, so the position has been tested rather than simply assembled. And somebody has to put their name to it, knowing the decision will be looked at again in two years by people who already know how it turned out.

That last one is the whole thing. Accountability is what converts analysis into a decision, and it is the part no system supplies, because a system cannot be embarrassed and cannot be sued and will not be in the room when it goes wrong.

So the sequence runs the other way round. Better decisions start with somebody prepared to be accountable for the answer, and insight is what they use to get there.
