## 1. The position in one paragraph

Kilkee discharges unscreened, untreated sewage to sea. Nobody disputes that has to end. Uisce Éireann holds a ten year permission, granted 11 June 2025 by An Coimisiún Pleanála under ACP 321258, for a preliminary and primary treatment plant on a circa 4.5 hectare coastal site at Foohagh and Kilkee Lower. Clare County Council had refused it. The Commission's own inspector recommended refusal. The Commission granted anyway. Six residents challenged by judicial review, 2025 JR 1205. Humphreys J dismissed the challenge in July 2026, reportedly on the basis that quashing the permission would be disproportionate given the urgent need to stop raw sewage discharge. The residents have applied for leave to appeal. Construction has not started and the land is not yet assembled.

## 2. The six questions, answered as far as the evidence allows

### Q1. Why did the Commission reject its inspector's recommendation?

**Unanswered. This is the highest value single document in the case.**

What is established (Tier B): the inspector recommended refusal; the Commission granted; residents argued in the judicial review that the Commission had not given adequate reasons for departing from the inspector.

What matters legally: a planning decision maker is entitled to disagree with its inspector. The obligation is to give reasons of sufficient quality that an interested party can understand why, and can assess whether a legal challenge is available. That is the *Connelly v An Bord Pleanála* [2018] IESC 31 standard. So the ground is never "they disagreed with the inspector", it is "the stated reasons for disagreeing are inadequate".

The Direction is the document that either satisfies that standard or does not. Get the Direction and the Amended Direction from ACP 321258. Read them against the inspector's stated grounds of refusal, point by point. If the Direction deals with visual amenity and capacity in a sentence each while the inspector devoted substantive analysis to them, there is an arguable reasons deficit. If the Direction engages properly, the ground is dead and the campaign should be told so.

The existence of an **Amended** Order and an **Amended** Direction is itself worth examining. Amendments to a Board Order are usually corrections under section 146A of the Planning and Development Act 2000, which permits correction of clerical errors only. If anything of substance was changed by amendment rather than by decision, that is a jurisdictional point. Tier D, speculative, but cheap to check and occasionally decisive.

### Q2. Is the 7,926 summer design population defensible?

**Probably yes as a planning matter, and probably not challengeable as a legal matter, but the units are being confused in public debate.**

Population equivalent is an organic load unit, not a headcount. A design of circa 7,926 p.e. over a thirty year horizon is not the same claim as "the plant serves 7,926 people". The campaign's figure of 20,000 is a peak visitor number. Those two numbers cannot be compared directly, and the public argument is currently comparing them directly. That weakens the campaign with anyone technical and strengthens it with anyone who is not.

The real question is narrower and sharper: what peak flow, in litres per second, does the plant accept before the emergency storage of circa 951 cubic metres fills, and what happens then. That is a hydraulic question, not a population question. It is answered in the hydraulic loading calculations on the Council file and in the inspector's assessment.

Note that Clare County Council refused partly on this ground. Reported refusal reasoning includes that, given significant seasonal variation in Kilkee's population, it had not been demonstrated that the plant could manage the loading (Tier B, Clare FM, Limerick Leader, Echo Live). So the Council, the statutory planning authority, was not satisfied. The Commission was. That divergence needs the two documents side by side.

Legally, a disagreement about design population is a merits disagreement. A court will not substitute its own view. It becomes a legal ground only if the assessment was irrational in the *O'Keeffe* or *Meadows* sense, or if a mandatory standard was misapplied. Do not let the campaign spend money on this as a standalone ground.

### Q3. What does primary treatment actually achieve?

Preliminary treatment is screening: rags, wipes, grit, gross solids. Primary treatment is settlement: solids drop out in tanks and are removed as sludge (Tier B, Uisce Éireann via Clare FM).

What that gives Kilkee: removal of visible sewage matter, roughly half to two thirds of suspended solids, and on typical performance somewhere around a quarter to a third of biochemical oxygen demand. What it does not give: biological treatment of dissolved organic load, nutrient removal, and, critically for a bathing water town, it delivers no reliable pathogen reduction. Primary settlement is not disinfection. E. coli and intestinal enterococci reductions from primary treatment alone are modest.

That last point is the one to press. Kilkee's public case for the plant rests heavily on bathing water quality. A preliminary and primary plant will substantially improve the *appearance* and gross solids position at Intrinsic Bay. Whether it materially improves *bathing water microbiology* at Kilkee's beaches depends on the outfall's dispersion modelling, not on the tanks. Get the outfall modelling. If the modelling shows compliance is achieved by dilution and dispersion rather than by treatment, that is a legitimate and defensible engineering position, and it should be stated plainly rather than left for the campaign to characterise.

Note the treatment percentages above are general engineering ranges, Tier D. Do not quote them externally. Replace them with the figures in the application's process design before this goes anywhere.

### Q4. How does primary treatment reconcile with the EPA record and the revised Directive?

**This is the strongest technical and regulatory line in the entire case, and it is the one that has been least well argued in public.**

Established (Tier B, EUR-Lex):

- Under Directive 91/271/EEC Article 7, discharges to coastal waters from agglomerations below 10,000 p.e. require only "appropriate treatment". Secondary treatment is not mandated. This is the legal basis on which a primary plant at circa 7,926 p.e. discharging to coastal water is lawful today.
- Directive (EU) 2024/3019, the recast, was adopted 27 November 2024 and published 12 December 2024. It extends secondary treatment to all discharges from agglomerations of 1,000 p.e. and above.
- The general compliance date is 31 December 2035.
- A derogation to 31 December 2037 applies to agglomerations of 2,000 p.e. and above but below 10,000 p.e. discharging into coastal waters **which apply appropriate treatment in accordance with Article 7 of Directive 91/271/EEC on 1 January 2025**.

Now the analysis (Tier D, inference, must be verified against the operative Articles and against Irish transposition):

1. Kilkee sits in the 2,000 to below 10,000 p.e. coastal band. On its face the 2037 derogation is available to it.
2. But the derogation is conditional. It applies to agglomerations which **were applying appropriate treatment** on 1 January 2025. Kilkee applied **no treatment at all** on 1 January 2025. It discharged unscreened raw sewage. On a strict reading, Kilkee does not satisfy the condition and cannot claim the 2037 date. That would put it on 31 December 2035.
3. Either way, the plant now permitted delivers a treatment standard that becomes non compliant during the life of its own ten year permission. A permission granted in June 2025 runs to June 2035. The secondary treatment obligation bites in 2035 or 2037.
4. Therefore the correct question was never "is primary treatment lawful". It is: **was the site assessed for its capacity to accommodate the secondary treatment upgrade that is already legislated for?** A 4.5 hectare site may well accommodate it. But if the answer is that no such assessment was carried out, then the state has permitted a plant on a contested coastal site that must be built again, or substantially extended, within roughly a decade, on the same contested site, with a second planning process and a second round of opposition.

Separately, and supplied by John, not verified by Cuan: the 2024 Annual Environmental Report for licence D0078-01 is said to list construction and operation of a **secondary** treatment plant as a specified improvement with an expected completion date of 2027. If that is accurate, there is a direct inconsistency between what the EPA licence record commits to and what the planning permission delivers. That does not make the permission unlawful. Licences get amended. But it is a serious governance question about whether two arms of the state are working to the same specification, and it is the kind of inconsistency that reads very badly in public and in the Oireachtas.

This also gives independent weight to the objection made by Michael Duffy for the Dunlicky Road Residents Group, that "it is unconscionable, in this day and age, that a new WWTP would be designed for primary treatment only" and that "a 10 year planning application is cynical in the extreme in circumstances where Kilkee should have had secondary wastewater treatment since 2000" (Tier B, Limerick Leader, Clare Echo).

**Action: this is the line to develop.** It is factual, it is verifiable from public EU and EPA documents, it does not depend on visual taste, and it is the only argument that puts Uisce Éireann on the back foot on its own terrain.

### Q5. Were alternative inland sites genuinely assessed?

Established (Tier B): objectors proposed at least two alternatives, a disused quarry approximately 2 km further along Dunlicky Road, and the Lisdeen Recycling Centre, the former Kilkee dump, which is Council owned.

Not established: whether Uisce Éireann carried out a comparative site selection exercise, on what criteria, and whether those two sites were scored or dismissed.

Legal reality, and this is important because it is where community campaigns most often waste money: in Irish planning law an applicant is generally **not** obliged to demonstrate that the chosen site is the best site. Alternatives assessment is only a legal requirement where EIA applies, under Annex IV of the EIA Directive, and even then it is an obligation to describe the reasonable alternatives studied by the developer, not to prove optimality.

No EIAR was submitted here. So unless the campaign can establish that EIA should have been carried out (see Q6), the alternative sites argument is a planning merits argument that has already been heard and lost. It is emotionally the strongest argument in Kilkee and legally close to the weakest. Somebody should tell them that.

The one exception worth checking: if the site selection report exists on the Council file and materially misdescribes the alternatives, for example by asserting a constraint on the quarry site that is untrue, that is a different matter. It goes to whether the decision maker was materially misled. Worth an hour of reading, not worth a campaign.

### Q6. What grounds did the High Court reject, and is there a credible appeal route?

**Established (Tier B).** Residents argued that An Coimisiún Pleanála acted unlawfully in granting permission, that it failed to properly assess the project's environmental impacts including effects on the Kilkee Cliff Walk, and that it had not given adequate reasons for departing from its inspector. Humphreys J dismissed the challenge, concluding that given the urgent need to end discharge of untreated sewage and the strong public interest in the project, quashing the permission would be disproportionate.

**The critical ambiguity, and the thing to establish first.** "Disproportionate to quash" is discretionary language. There are two very different judgments hiding behind that phrase:

- **Reading A.** The court found no legal error. The proportionality remark is an additional observation. The appeal is then very difficult.
- **Reading B.** The court found one or more legal errors but declined to grant certiorari as a matter of discretion, on proportionality and public interest grounds.

If Reading B is correct, the appeal position changes completely. A court declining to quash a decision that it has found to be unlawful, in an environmental case, engages the EU law principles of effectiveness and of adequate and effective remedies, and Article 9(4) of the Aarhus Convention as given effect in EU law. Whether and when an Irish court may exercise discretion to refuse relief despite an established breach of an EU environmental directive is precisely the kind of question that qualifies as a point of general public importance. That is an appeal.

**Do not form a view on the appeal until the judgment has been read.** Everything turns on this distinction.

**The appeal route itself.** Two mechanisms, and they interact awkwardly:

- Section 50A(7) of the Planning and Development Act 2000 provides that no appeal lies from a High Court decision in planning judicial review except with the leave of the High Court, which requires the court to certify that the decision involves a point of law of exceptional public importance and that it is desirable in the public interest that an appeal be taken. This is a demanding filter and it is the applicants' own court that applies it.
- Article 34.5.4 of the Constitution allows a direct appeal from the High Court to the Supreme Court where the Supreme Court is satisfied there are exceptional circumstances warranting it, and that the matter is of general public importance or an appeal is necessary in the interests of justice. The Supreme Court decides this itself.

The relationship between the statutory bar in section 50A(7) and the constitutional leapfrog jurisdiction has been litigated and is not a clean question. Do not assert a settled position on it. It is a matter for the residents' counsel, and it is itself a candidate point of general public importance.

Reported position as at 8 August 2026: the residents have applied to the High Court seeking leave to appeal directly to the Supreme Court. A decision is expected in September 2026, along with outstanding matters on costs. No appeal has been accepted. Anyone stating otherwise is ahead of the record.

## 3. Grounds not yet properly examined, ranked by legal promise

These are the lines Cuan would test first against the primary documents. Tier D throughout. They are hypotheses for verification, not findings.

**1. EIA screening. Highest promise.**
No EIAR was submitted. Wastewater treatment plants become subject to mandatory EIA above a population equivalent threshold, and Kilkee at circa 7,926 p.e. sits below it. That means the project is sub threshold and required a screening determination. Sub threshold screening is one of the most frequently and most successfully challenged aspects of Irish planning decisions, because the screening must take account of the characteristics, location and sensitivity of the receiving environment, including proximity to designated sites and to landscapes of recognised value. Here: a coastal site adjoining a nationally promoted scenic route, a geological Natural Heritage Area at Foohagh Point, and European sites in the vicinity. Get the screening determination. Read whether it engaged with cumulative effects and with the sensitivity of the location, or whether it simply recited the threshold and stopped. Confirm the exact threshold class and figure from the Planning and Development Regulations 2001 Schedule 5 before relying on any of this.

**2. Material contravention of the Clare County Development Plan. High promise.**
Duffy contended that the pump station site is zoned for tourism, which does not permit utility use including wastewater infrastructure (Tier B, Limerick Leader). If that zoning position is correct, then the grant was a material contravention of the development plan. A decision maker may grant in material contravention, but only by expressly invoking section 37(2)(b) of the Planning and Development Act 2000 and identifying which of its limbs applies, with reasons. If the Commission granted without identifying the contravention and without invoking the section, that is a jurisdictional error, not a merits disagreement. Check the zoning objective for the pump station site in the current Clare County Development Plan, then check whether the Board Order and Direction address section 37(2)(b).

**3. Appropriate Assessment adequacy. Moderate to high promise.**
A Natura Impact Statement accompanied the application. Under Article 6(3) of the Habitats Directive, the competent authority may only grant consent where it has ascertained, on the basis of complete, precise and definitive findings, that there will be no adverse effect on the integrity of the European site concerned, with no reasonable scientific doubt remaining. That is a strict standard and Irish courts apply it strictly. The point of attack is the emergency overflow. If the NIS treats overflow events as exceptional and therefore does not model their effects on the marine European sites, that is a gap of the kind that has quashed permissions before. Read the NIS against the Commission's AA conclusion in the Board Order.

**4. Adequacy of reasons for departing from the inspector. Moderate promise.**
Covered at Q1. Depends entirely on the text of the Direction. Cheap to assess, worth doing first because it is one document.

**5. The Victoria Stream overflow. Factual question first, legal question second.**
Residents fear the Victoria Stream, which reaches Kilkee beach, will be used as an emergency overflow. Establish the fact before arguing the law. The permitted drawings will show whether there is any hydraulic connection from the works to the Victoria Stream, and the emergency storage sizing will show the return period at which storage is exceeded. If there is no connection, this claim is wrong and repeating it damages the campaign's credibility on the points where it is right. If there is a connection, it feeds directly into ground 3 above.

**6. Alternative sites. Low legal promise.** See Q5.

**7. Visual amenity and the cliff walk. No legal promise.** This is the emotional heart of the campaign and it is a pure merits question. The Council agreed with the residents. The Commission did not. That disagreement was lawfully resolved against them in June 2025. It cannot be relitigated.

## 4. Where each side is overstating

**The campaign overstates when it:**
- compares a peak visitor headcount of 20,000 against a design figure of 7,926 p.e. as though they were the same unit;
- describes the tanks as "40ft high, unsightly tanks of raw sewage, open to the elements, being agitated constantly", which conflates primary settlement with aerated biological treatment. Primary settlement tanks are not continuously agitated. If that description is wrong, Uisce Éireann will use it to discredit everything else the campaign says;
- asserted that sludge would be incinerated in Limerick. Uisce Éireann says it operates no such facility and that sludge goes to licensed off site facilities. Unless the campaign can evidence otherwise, that claim should be dropped;
- treats the visual and site selection arguments as legal arguments. They are not, and they are consuming the campaign's remaining money.

**Uisce Éireann overstates when it:**
- presents the project as delivering wastewater treatment for Kilkee without stating plainly that the standard delivered is below the secondary standard that EU law will require within the life of the permission;
- relies on the improvement over the status quo as the answer to the adequacy question. Everything is an improvement over untreated discharge. That is not the test for whether this is the right plant;
- characterises the campaign's material as "a significant level of misinformation, inaccuracies and factually incorrect" without distinguishing the claims that are genuinely wrong, such as the Limerick incineration claim, from the claims that are substantively right, such as the point about the secondary treatment standard.

**Both sides are silent on the same thing:** what happens in 2035 or 2037, and whether this site can take the upgrade.

## 5. Cuan's assessment

The campaign has lost the argument it has been running and has not yet run the argument it could win.

It has been fighting on location, visual impact and site selection. Those are planning merits. They were decided against it by the Commission in June 2025, and the High Court will not and did not reopen them. Continuing to lead with them is why the campaign is out of legal road.

The argument it has barely developed is the regulatory timing argument. Kilkee is being given a plant built to a standard that EU law will render insufficient during the currency of its own permission, on a site that the state may then have to expand on the same contested clifftop. That argument is factual, checkable from public documents, and uncomfortable for Uisce Éireann in a way that no amount of landscape objection is. It also reframes the campaign from "not here" to "build it once, build it right", which is a far stronger public and political position and is consistent with what the campaign already says it wants.

On the legal side, everything turns on one unread document. If Humphreys J found no error, the appeal is close to hopeless and the honest advice is to stop spending. If he found error and declined relief on discretionary grounds, there is a genuine point of general public importance about the limits of judicial discretion to withhold remedies in EU environmental cases, and that is worth an appeal.

The practical position is also worth noting. The permission is intact but the project is not. Land is not assembled, a CPO is in train under ACP 322828, and the CPO carries its own objection and oral hearing process with its own timetable. Whatever happens in September, the project is not imminent.

**Provisional conclusion, unchanged in substance from the initial view but now sharper:** the need is settled, the location is lost, and the treatment standard is the live question. That is where the remaining effort belongs.

## 6. What would change this assessment

- The Direction showing thin or formulaic reasoning on the inspector's refusal grounds. Would raise ground 4 materially.
- An EIA screening determination that does not engage with the sensitivity of the location. Would move ground 1 to the front.
- A High Court judgment establishing legal error followed by discretionary refusal of relief. Would make the appeal serious.
- Confirmation that the D0078-01 Annual Environmental Report commits to secondary treatment by 2027. Would turn the Q4 analysis from an argument into a documented contradiction between two state bodies.
- A site layout showing no room for a future secondary treatment stage. Would be the single most damaging fact for Uisce Éireann in the whole file.
