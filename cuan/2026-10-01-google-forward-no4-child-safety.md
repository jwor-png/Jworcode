# Google Forward No. 04: child safety and AI

Summaries are paraphrased. This is a Google publication promoting its own products, so treat it as an industry position, not independent evidence. Statistics come from research Google commissioned and should be cited as such.

## Core framing

- Google's three pillars: protect young people online, respect families' choices, empower young people to learn and explore.
- The central reframe, repeated throughout: protect children in the digital world, not from it.
- Approach: safeguards on by default, products safer by design, firm protections for under-18s, plus education.
- Editorial by Vanessa Hartley, Head of Google Ireland. Google says it has worked with Barnardos in Ireland on online safety training for over 300,000 children since 2019.

## Policy direction (most important for Childen)

- **Safety by default, set by the maker.** Ursula von der Leyen, speaking at the EU Summit on AI and Children, compares platforms to car manufacturers: whoever builds the product is responsible for its safety.
- **Flip the defaults.** EU Commissioner Michael McGrath argues protections should be built in by default, with parents able to adjust them.
- **Ecosystem, not bans.** Eugenia Roccella, Italian Minister for Family, Natality and Equal Opportunities, argues bans alone fail and calls for an ecosystem involving families, schools, institutions and platforms, scaled to age and maturity.
- **Rights-based design.** Niall Muldoon, Ireland's Ombudsman for Children (quoted from The Irish Times), says products reaching children should be designed around their rights, health and welfare rather than engagement and profit. Children want to be protected, not excluded.
- **Skills over fear.** Jeffrey DeMarco, Save the Children, calls for a competency-based approach focused on resilience and digital wellbeing rather than harms.
- **Common ground.** Stephen Balkam, Family Online Safety Institute, calls for cooperation across sectors, institutions and generations.

## Design standards platforms now claim

- Age-tiered experiences: YouTube Kids for under-13s; supervised pre-teen accounts with three content settings starting at 9+; supervised teen accounts.
- Defaults for under-18s: autoplay off, uploads private, take-a-break reminders every 60 minutes and bedtime reminders on by default for 13 to 17s, SafeSearch on for signed-in under-18s.
- Limits on repeated recommendation of content that can be harmful in volume, such as content idealising body standards.
- Google states its teen AI products include guardrails against emotional reliance and against encouraging bullying or harassment.
- Parental tools via Family Link: screen time limits, school-time mode, app controls, location, account management.
- Mental health shelves surface age-appropriate information from trusted sources such as national health services and charities.

**Implication for Childen:** these are now the baseline. A child protection architecture needs to meet or exceed them, particularly on AI emotional reliance and age-appropriate defaults.

## Expert insight: Jessica Taylor Piotrowski

- Professor at the University of Amsterdam, director of the Center for Research on Children, Adolescents and the Media, member of YouTube's Youth and Families Advisory Committee.
- Argues the debate is too protective: adolescents need some risk and real content, and blocking everything fails to meet them where they are.
- Parents want a trusted space with age-appropriate experiences and built-in protections.
- The hardest decisions are grey areas, such as how mental health and suicide topics are handled for young people.
- Just-in-time guidance while children use technology works better than classroom lessons alone. She describes a Swiss cheese model where policymakers, companies, teachers and parents each hold a layer.
- Biggest research gap: how to build digital competence at scale, and which business models support healthy environments for children rather than profit alone.
- Her advisory committee meets fortnightly and reviews unreleased products, which is a governance model worth noting.

## Expert insight: Garth Graham M.D.

- YouTube's Head of Health. Uses a bike-riding analogy: freedom expands with age.
- Recommends a family media agreement built with children, then enforced through tools so the device, not the parent, ends the session.
- Key habit: open dialogue about what children watch and why.

## Research figures (Google-commissioned)

- Source: The Future Report 2026, commissioned by Google and delivered by Livity, surveying over 16,000 European teenagers; YouTube Impact Report 2025 by Oxford Economics.
- Reported: a majority of teens use AI for learning and creative tasks at least a couple of times a week; roughly a fifth report chatting to AI as a friend; most say they check the trustworthiness of information; most would ask a parent for help with an online scam.
- Use with attribution only.

## Education programmes

- Be Internet Awesome, called Be Internet Legends in Ireland, funded through Google.org.
- Delivery partners across Europe include Barnardos (Ireland), Save the Children (Nordics), e-Enfance (France), Fondazione Mondo Digitale (Italy), Pro Juventute (Switzerland) and others.
- Family Guide to AI covers five messages for children: AI is a helpful assistant, AI isn't human, AI makes mistakes, your best ideas come from you, and use AI safely by never sharing private information.

## Cross-cutting insight

The policy consensus has moved to safety by design, set by default, scaled by age, with children's rights at the centre and empowerment alongside protection. Childen should position itself as the architecture that delivers this consensus, independent of any single platform, which is something Google cannot credibly claim for itself.
