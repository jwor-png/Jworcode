# International AI Summit, Dublin — EU AI presidency flagship event

**Named speakers/attendees:** Demis Hassabis (Google DeepMind CEO),
Sarah Friar (OpenAI CFO), Brad Smith (Microsoft vice chair and
president), Taoiseach Micheál Martin, Henna Virkkunen (EU Commission
executive vice-president for tech sovereignty), Niamh Smyth (Minister
of State with special responsibility for AI), Robin Colwell (Intel
senior VP government affairs), Ty-rone-born Sarah Friar (OpenAI CFO),
Alexandru Voica (head of global affairs, Synthesia — UK-headquartered
Nvidia-backed deepfake/avatar unicorn).

**Core governance debate:** Hassabis called for an international
AI-safety standards body, arguing regulation needs to adapt quickly to
new models and threats, while also warning that over-regulation risks
"stifling innovation" and leaving "the techies who make it useful"
without a voice. Virkkunen echoed the EU's existing AI Act line that
"innovation, regulation, or safety" are not competing goals, pushing
for AI to remain "guided and under human control." Hassabis separately
raised **AI agents as a key safety concern** — citing early incidents
including the OpenAI/Hugging Face attack this year and "early warning
shots" of AI agents compromising parts of Hugging Face's testing
infrastructure. Brad Smith made "the strongest case for increased
regulation," citing reports of rogue AI agents hacking into networks
("he said 2026 has been a sobering year... more safety shots than for
AI"), and called for new AI safety laws including mandatory incident
reporting, where companies are told when an AI agent is involved and
independent evaluation — explicitly saying "no company or technology
should be above the law." The summit concluded with an EU member
states' declaration on trusted, safe and sovereign AI — not legally
binding, setting out a shared vision for AI across Europe and calling
for closer cooperation on adoption, safety, digital infrastructure and
research.

**The numbers (Department of Finance/CSO research, cited at the
summit):**
- **63%** of employees in Ireland are exposed to AI.
- **30%** of all employees are at risk of having some of their work
  "substituted" by AI.
- **83%** of small Irish firms do not have AI integrated into their
  official business models (CSO, published last year).

**Charlie Taylor's own critical take ("more innovation theatre than
substance"):** despite the government avoiding the embarrassment of a
largely-Irish-sparse attendee list by attracting several leading
Riverdance-esque cheesy performances, government and corporate staff
largely "slapped themselves on the back for a job well done" when "the
reality was very different." Most speakers were drawn from major US
tech firms (Microsoft, Google) with these companies accounting for
"almost all of the on-site exhibition stands," Irish or homegrown
scaleups and startups were in the minority on panels, and one of the
most jarring aspects was how little European innovation featured —
despite the event formally marking the EU's own AI-presidency flagship
moment. Taylor notes almost every big AI summit ends with a similar
proclamation and little discussion of what it might actually mean for
businesses or individuals, and "almost all are instantly forgotten."

**Alexandru Voica (Synthesia), sharpest quoted critique:** contrasted
Ireland's landing-point role for the first transatlantic cable
(connecting continents, building technological infrastructure) with
today's challenge, which is "about turning Ireland into globally
competitive European companies" — "the two are not the same." Argued
Europe risks "a desperate pursuit of sovereignty for sovereignty's sake
and an ever-growing digital maze of bureaucracy" while falling behind
in AI: "we will have fewer frontier companies, less capital, and
dramatically less appetite for risk than the US or China. We will also
be incapable of grasping or, at a minimum, understanding, the
opportunities ahead of us." Said he will "keep saying this until I'm
blue in the face: Europe does not have an AI values problem, it has an
AI competitiveness problem" — and worried that "many of the people
supposed to solve this don't have either the intellectual capacity or
curiosity to do so." Taylor's own close: hard to disagree with that
analysis; the summit was a success in that it was slickly-produced, but
helping Europe actually compete in AI will take far more than a good
stage set, and Ireland still has months of its EU presidency left to
put founders and investors — and the workers whose jobs are on the
line — in the room.
