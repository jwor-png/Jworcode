## 1. "KPMG study on spread of AI falls victim to hallucinations" (Elizabeth Burden-Brat, London; Stephen Foley, New York)

**Strong, directly on-point addition to Meridian's AI Strategy &
Governance domain** — a genuinely significant story: a **Big Four
professional services firm's own AI report was itself wrong, because
it was built on AI hallucinations.**

- **The report:** KPMG's October report, "Redefining Excellence in the
  Age of Agentic AI," made numerous false claims about AI use by named
  organisations — **UBS, the UK's National Health Service, Swiss
  Federal Railways, and Transport for London.**
- **How the errors were caught:** the inaccuracies were identified as
  AI hallucinations by **research group GPTZero**, and verified by the
  FT directly with each named organisation.
- **UBS's own response, directly quotable:** told the FT the
  assertions were **"factually incorrect."** KPMG's report had claimed
  UBS "integrates AI into investment advisory, risk management and
  compliance monitoring" via "agents operating within a composable
  platform co-developed with Microsoft" — UBS flatly denied this.
- **Swiss Federal Railways (SBB):** KPMG claimed SBB offered AI agents
  that "help users plan, book and optimise journeys based on
  preferences, real-time conditions and carbon impact, turning SBB
  into a holistic mobility orchestrator." SBB confirmed the assertion
  was **"not accurate."**
- **Transport for London:** KPMG claimed TfL was using AI agents "to
  predict and manage congestion, personalise commuter updates and
  co-ordinate multimodal transport" — TfL said the assertion was
  **"misleading."**
- **NHS Greater Manchester:** a KPMG claim that NHS Greater Manchester
  uses AI agents to predict hospital readmissions, triage patients and
  automate referrals **"doesn't really align"** with the press release
  it appeared to be based on, per an NHS spokesperson.
- **KPMG's own response:** after being alerted, said it would ask
  GPTZero to remove the false claims, and the firm pulled the report
  from some of its websites on the Thursday following the discovery.
- **Not an isolated incident:** the piece notes this is **"the latest
  in a number of agentic AI hallucinations in reports by professional
  services firms,"** following the retraction last month of a separate
  study over fake footnotes and other errors, also identified by
  GPTZero.

**Cross-reference:** this is one of the strongest, most directly

## 2. "AI's disruptive force is playing out in the investment world" (Katie Martin, The Long View)

- Core framing: AI technology's "winner-takes-all characteristics" are
  fundamentally resetting how investors allocate and diversify risk
  across every major asset class — described by one commentator as the
  **"equitification" of corporate debt.**
- **Bond market concentration risk:** Richard Woolnough (M&G
  Investments) notes bond portfolios are typically subject to strict
  diversification limits, capped exposure to any single issuer — but
  "well below levels commonly seen in equities," meaning even highly
  rated issuers can struggle to find incremental buyers when supply
  increases sharply, making it "difficult to absorb" issuance at the
  current AI-driven scale.
- **The rush of IPO/public listings from AI-flavoured companies:**
  described as "the most high-profile and obvious element of this
  revolution" — **Elon Musk's SpaceX launched on to public markets
  with fanfare**, with Alphabet and others reportedly readying "fully
  two decades of shrinkage in US stock markets" worth of new equity
  issuance.
- **Diversification becoming "increasingly tricky"** as the AI
  ecosystem gobbles up a growing slice of both debt and stock markets —
  investors forced to recalibrate as new debt issuance and even
  "monster-sized borrowers" treat major benchmarks as safe bets by
  default, simply because of their index weighting.
- **Kevin Warsh (new Federal Reserve chair)** believes AI capital
  spending is on the cusp of becoming a **disinflationary force** that
  could justify a path toward rate cuts — a view that "may well be
  proved right in time," per the piece.

**Cross-reference:** a fourth/fifth independent voice (after Gillen,
the BoE FPC piece, and Alan O'Sullivan, all already logged) converging
on the AI-infrastructure-financing-risk thread, this time from the
bond-market/index-concentration angle specifically rather than pure
capex numbers — worth holding alongside the existing thread as the
"market structure" variant of the same underlying concern.

## 3. "AI is revolutionising the stock market" (Robert Armstrong, FT Big Read, dated 4 June 2026 per clipping)

An older piece (June 2026) but data-dense and directly relevant —
worth logging now despite the date gap, given how well it extends the
same thread.

- **Core question posed:** "Big Tech no longer prints money; it needs
  it. Are the companies irrational in abandoning old business models
  for something less profitable and more uncertain — and what happens
  when confidence dips?"
- **The "de-equitisation to re-equitisation" reversal:** for the past
  decade, Alphabet, Meta and Microsoft have been **cash machines**,
  buying back almost **$800bn of their own shares** over 10 years
  (Amazon rarely does buybacks). Wall Street analysts expect capital
  investment at the four majors to **rise sixfold between 2023 and
  2027, to a staggering $815bn** — with free cash flow expected to
  **fall 70%** as a result.
- **AI-linked tech spending now makes up more than a third of US
  business investment** (information-processing equipment, software
  and data-centre structures as % of business investment) — a genuine,
  broad-based macroeconomic shift, not just a Big Tech phenomenon.
- **SpaceX's 2026 IPO towers over past US-listed IPOs by implied
  market value** — ranked above Alibaba (2014), Facebook (2012), Uber
  (2019), per the chart data shown.
- **Named skeptical framing, directly quotable:** Robert Buckland
  (strategist, Citigroup, who popularised "de-equitisation" as a
  concept) — "When a correction happens — which it will — it will be
  harder for these guys to prop up their stock than it was before, as
  when Meta bought lots of its own shares back after its stock crashed
  [in 2021-2022]."
- **The "Giffen goods" risk flagged by Ian Harnett (Absolute Strategy
  Research):** "When share prices go up enough, they become Giffen
  goods [where demand rises with price]. You get the wrong cost of
  capital, people pay themselves a lot of money and they over-invest.
  It becomes an abusive relationship between investors and companies."
- **Noah Weisberger (BCA Research)** notes historically "IPO waves are
  usually followed by more muted returns and valuation pressures" —
  not necessarily that returns turn negative, but that it suggests the
  most rapid phase of a rally is likely already behind.
- **Closing framing, worth holding directly alongside the other
  pieces in this thread:** "the break-up will be messy soon enough,
  the economy and the market will survive, but will be left with
  scars... after the US housing bubble popped, residential
  construction never fully recovered... after the dotcom bubble burst,
  stock valuations kept falling for 10 years."

**Cross-reference:** this is the single most data-dense piece in the
entire AI-capex/bubble thread now logged across this market intel
series — the $815bn capex forecast, the 70% free-cash-flow decline
forecast, and the "a third of US business investment" figure are all
