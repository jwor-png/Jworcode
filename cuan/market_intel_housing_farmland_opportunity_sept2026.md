# Market intel — "Could housing be the next big thing in farming?"

**Source:** Irish Independent, Farming p.5 (Comment), Tuesday 22
September 2026, Darragh McCullough (runs a mixed farm enterprise in
Meath, Elmgroveflowerfarm.ie). REPORTED — columnist's own account and
figures cited from Glenveagh/industry sources; not independently
verified by Cuan.

## What it says
McCullough attended the National Ploughing Championships for the first
time in five years and noted Glenveagh Properties (a €1bn plc, one of
Ireland's biggest housebuilders, building nearly 3,000 units/year with
ambitions to scale further) had a stand there for the first time,
targeting both students (recruitment, given an estimated 4,000-5,000
current construction job vacancies and a forecast need to hire 40,000
people over the next decade) and **landowners directly**, offering
partnership-style land deals.

**Scale of opportunity cited:** Government-mandated development plans
over the next 14 years mean an estimated **70,000 acres** of land is
likely to be zoned for building by local authorities; not all will be
built on, but **50,000ac will be needed** over that period to meet the
Government's target of 50,000 homes/year. At a basic valuation of
**€200,000/acre**, that represents a **€10bn opportunity for
landowners**.

**The pressure point:** every farming acre that gets rezoned but sits
undeveloped is now exposed to the **Residential Zoned Land Tax (RZLT)**
at a minimum of €6,000 per zoned acre per year (cross-reference: the
RZLT article already logged, `market_intel_farm_land_tax_succession_cap_batch.md`)
— McCullough's framing: landowners "can't really afford to just bury
their heads in the sand and pretend nothing has changed," since county
councils are highly unlikely to de-zone land once earmarked for
development.

**Case example cited:** George and Kate Williams, farming near Ratoath,
have seen nearly 400 houses built on their farm over 25 years, "no
regrets" — reinvested in more farmland, enjoy new neighbours and the
financial security the windfall created.

**Recruitment angle (Glenveagh):** apprentices start on pay packages
similar to college graduates after a 3-4 year apprenticeship, moving
into full-time roles paying over €40,000/year, effectively paid to
train — pitched as an attractive alternative to four years of college
for farmers' sons/daughters ("their practical can-do attitude," echoing
Michael O'Leary's stated preference for hiring farmers' children at
Ryanair).
