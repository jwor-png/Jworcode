# Market Intelligence — Irish Independent "Farming Independent" Legal Q&A,
8 Sept 2026

Newspaper clipping photographed by John, 9 Sept 2026. Legal advice
column, Mary Frances Fahy (solicitor and tax consultant, principal of
Fahy Neilan Solicitors, Ballaghaderreen, Co Roscommon) — "Can my
mother sell estate land to my brother below market value?"

## The scenario
Reader is one of three children of a deceased farmer. Father's will
appointed the mother as sole executor; the brother inherited the home
place and farms it; a separate parcel of estate land remains
undistributed. The brother now wants to buy that parcel from the
estate. The reader and her sister (both beneficiaries, both with
mortgages/financial commitments of their own) believe their mother —
under pressure from the brother — is willing to sell it to him below
market value, without an independent valuation or open-market process.

## Key legal points from the answer
- Under **Section 50 of the Succession Act**, an executor holding
  probate has the power to sell estate assets, but must have regard to
  the wishes of the beneficiaries entitled to the land.
- An executor's primary duties: act **impartially** between
  beneficiaries, and avoid **conflicts of interest** — an executor with
  a personal interest in an asset should consider renouncing that role
  for it.
- Standard practice when a family member wants to buy from an estate:
  obtain **two, often three, independent valuations** from registered
  auctioneers so beneficiaries can agree the price is fair.
- Selling to a family member is not itself a breach of duty — selling
  **below market value without the other beneficiaries' consent** is.
- Recommended sequence for the aggrieved beneficiaries: get their own
  independent valuation → instruct a solicitor → solicitor writes to
  the estate's solicitors formally opposing any sale below market
  value and setting out personal liability for the executor if she
  proceeds regardless (she can be pursued personally for the shortfall
  between sale price and market value).
- **Removing an executor is very difficult** — the columnist notes
  that in almost 20 years of practice she has never seen an order
  granted to remove an executor; burden of proof sits with the
  applicant, and grounds like "delay" routinely fail if the executor
  can justify the time taken.
- Litigation is costly, risky, and a last resort — mediation is raised
  as a middle option before court.
