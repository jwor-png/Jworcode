# "On Wall Street: The coming futures market in AI compute" — Marc Rubinstein

Source: FT Weekend, Companies & Markets section, Marc Rubinstein (former hedge fund manager, author of the Net Interest newsletter). VERIFIED — clearly legible, named-author financial analysis piece.

## What it says

**CME Group** (Chicago Mercantile Exchange, which already trades soyabeans, precious metals, lean hogs, crude oil, interest rates) plans to introduce a new commodity to its roster: **compute**, which it dubs "the new currency for the AI economy." Pete Keavey (CME executive responsible), announcing the product in August: "Just as oil fuelled the 20th-century economy and evolved from spot trading into a global derivatives market, our futures contracts will now turn compute into a standardised, tradeable commodity." BlackRock CEO Larry Fink (May 2026): "A new asset class will be buying futures of compute."

**The market size case:** Boston Consulting Group estimates the AI compute market will climb from $360bn in 2025 to roughly $2.3tn in 2030 — if CME captures even a fraction of that flow, it's a lucrative new revenue stream for the exchange.

**Why a market existing doesn't guarantee it's tradeable:** research on the origins of futures markets finds between two-thirds and three-quarters of new contracts fail to attract and sustain a profitable level of trading volume. For compute to flourish the way oil derivatives have, several conditions must be met:

1. **Underlying prices need to be volatile enough to make hedging worthwhile.** Speculators may supply liquidity, but it's commercial hedgers that give futures markets their economic purpose. On this test, compute's case is fairly strong — long lead times bringing new capacity online can make pricing quite volatile. Example: Oracle's Project Jupiter data centre build (due online 2028) has faced delays over a planned natural-gas pipeline and legal challenges over water/air-quality permits. More generally, when chip bottlenecks were tightest in early 2024, hourly rental rates for a Nvidia H100 chip spiked as high as $8, before falling below $2 by late 2025 — that kind of variance is exactly what participants may want to hedge.

2. **Participants need to agree on a standardised contract.** When oil futures listed in 1983, they referenced West Texas Intermediate, with Cushing, Oklahoma as the physical delivery point — though WTI is only a small share of global crude output, it became the pricing benchmark for the rest of the market. By contrast, pioneers of bandwidth trading in 1999 struggled to converge on a standard; reliability/quality/bandwidth services varied too widely, and attempts to fashion a market collapsed with its champion, **Enron**. CME Group, working with index provider Silicon Data, has chosen Nvidia's **H100 and B200 chips** as the reference for its contracts. Per Silicon Data: capacity on a B200 can currently be rented for $5.86/hour; $2.77 on the older H100. CME plans to list contracts reflecting the future value of these rental rates, going out 36 months.

**Complications:** even a "GPU-hour" isn't fully standardised — performance varies with cluster configuration, networking, software and location, and rapid obsolescence fragments the market further (an H100-hour isn't interchangeable with a B200-hour, requiring separate contracts for each). There's also no consensus yet on how to measure even the rental rates themselves — a rival index provider, Ornn, has its own benchmark, and the two don't always align; until a consensus forms, a derivatives market may be slow to build.

**Concentration risk:** a vibrant derivatives market requires a diverse group of buyers and sellers. Onion futures were explicitly banned in the US in 1958 after two traders cornered the market. Compute is unlikely to be cornered in quite the same way, but is already highly concentrated — Nvidia dominates chip supply, hyperscalers control much of the capacity, and a handful of AI laboratories account for much of the demand.

**Rubinstein's closing assessment:** CME's compute futures will attract plenty of attention when they list — the price of compute increasingly bears on the economics of AI, the value of its infrastructure, and the vast investment boom surrounding it, giving investors good reason to want a benchmark for it. He draws a comparison to the ABX subprime index, which "illuminated a previously opaque corner of mortgage finance and became a focal point for the unwind that followed" — whether compute futures validate the AI boom or puncture it, they may ultimately matter more as a signal than as a market.
