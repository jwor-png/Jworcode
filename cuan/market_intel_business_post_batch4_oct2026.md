# Business Post batch 4, 11 Oct 2026

## 1. "TEKenable: Turning transformation into action" — Nick Connors, Group Chief Executive, TEKenable

TEKenable (founded 2002, 200 employees, software services company
focused on the public sector) — **a second, fuller profile of a
company already logged once before** (`market_intel_ai_health_medtech_feature_sept2026.md`,
27 Sept, where TEKenable's "admin-burden/scaling-gap thesis" was
briefly noted). This piece gives much more detail.

- **Core framing — AI's impact comes in two aspects:** (1) point
  solutions (data capture, hand-offs, automating spreadsheets/legacy
  systems), and (2) larger, organisation-wide implementation.
  "Reaching the point where AI delivers for a business is far more
  than just plug and play."
- **Connors's own sequencing advice, directly quotable and
  structured:**
  1. **Understand where AI could/should be applied** before investing
     and implementing — "we see a lot of people diving into solutions
     without looking at the problem they have right now."
  2. **Data readiness is the second big issue.** "If your data isn't
     in a state of readiness, pointing AI at it will make a bigger
     problem" — echoes the Datapac/Stryve point already logged
     yesterday, now a third independent source making the same
     argument.
  3. **Avoid over-scoping or under-promising** — "AI may be
     inescapable but it's not a catch-all solution... they must treat
     it like any other tool and find its best use. Otherwise they
     might end up taking a sledgehammer to a walnut."
- **Practical implementation approach described:** flip the
  organisation from its leadership down to the business, rather than
  bottom-up — run workshops bringing people up to speed, build a
  holistic organisation-wide understanding (not just IT/technical
  experts), then identify where AI possibly could/should be applied,
  with KPIs (key performance indicators) measuring impact.
- **On replacing jobs — explicit reassurance framing:** "the point
  where these two approaches can succeed is far more than just plug
  and play... it's not replacing jobs, it's about allowing the people
  to do more higher-value work" — reaching customers where they are,
  letting AI handle repetitive/mundane tasks so IT or business teams
  can step in on more complex, judgement-driven implementation.
- **Impact happens incrementally, not as "one big bang."**

**Cross-reference:** reinforces (third independent source now) the
"data readiness before AI" thesis already logged twice yesterday
(Datapac's Mallon, Stryve's Bayer) — strong, repeated convergence
across multiple named Irish AI-services companies, worth treating as
an established, well-evidenced pattern for Meridian's own AI
Adoption Offering framework, not just one company's opinion.

## 2. "Digital transformation starts with curiosity, not technology" — Dr Breda O'Dwyer, Head of Department of Organisation and Professional Development, MTU

- **Core argument:** digital transformation requires organisations to
  rethink how they work and develop "a culture of curiosity" —
  overcoming fear of the unknown is the real unlock, not the
  technology itself. "Take courage and, I dare say it as simply as
  possible, go back to being a child and develop that sense of
  curiosity."
- **Practical starting method:** don't attempt overnight
  transformation — start small, map the business end-to-end ("what is
  our process? What do we do in our organisation?"), then ask whether
  to digitalise the back end or front end, and which aspect generates
  the most value to reinvest.
- **Must involve the whole organisation, not just IT:** technology
  should be used to uncover inefficiencies, improve processes and
  generate better information — not treated as an IT-department
  responsibility alone.
- **"Data is power... data is not negative. By having that data in our
  hands, we are more informed to make decisions. Technology on its own
  won't help. It'll just present the data. Without decisions being
  made, it's useless."** — directly reinforces the
  governance-and-judgement theme already well tracked across this
  whole batch of articles.
- **Smaller/micro businesses specifically flagged as acutely
  challenged:** they often lack internal expertise to assess
  technologies, implement them and build confidence using them —
  "support and facilitation are therefore critical... for many, the
  issue is not the willingness to do this, it's the time, it's the
  money and expertise are already stretched."
- **Analogy used twice in the piece, worth quoting directly:** "You
  need somebody who's going to hold your hand until you actually gain
  the trust. It's just like learning how to swim or learning how to
  walk. It can be a bit shaky and you'll be a bit dodgy, but bit by bit
  you get to, 'Okay, let go. I'm okay here. I can do this.' That's
  exactly what it's like. It is a literacy journey." — directly
  usable, highly reusable framing for Golden Generation/Velocity AI
  training material given the emphasis on AI literacy specifically.
- **Named sector examples of digital transformation potential:**
  agriculture (farmers managing time/resources), healthcare
  (professional time focused where most needed).
- **Advice for business leaders:** resist tackling everything at once
  — find a mentor/trusted person to support the journey; "don't try
  and run the marathon. Maybe do the park run first and then go for
  the marathon. Get comfortable with the unknown."

**Cross-reference:** the "AI literacy as a journey, need a trusted
hand-holder" framing is an excellent, directly reusable metaphor for
Golden Generation's own AI-literacy positioning and Velocity AI's
executive training programme — independent, academically-sourced
(MTU) validation of exactly the role Cuan/Meridian already plays for
John and the role Velocity AI training plays for client organisations.

## 3. "Can the Budget create conditions for more Irish businesses to scale?" (David Shanahan, Partner/Head of Tax & Legal, Deloitte Ireland)

- **The proposed €1bn Strategic Investment Programme**, under the
  Ireland Strategic Investment Fund (ISIF), to be administered with
  Enterprise Ireland — intended to help scale Irish businesses
  internationally, but Shanahan argues the harder question is
  **design**, not just capital size: "patient capital can give an
  Irish company room to invest, hire, explore and enter markets" —
  but the programme needs genuine differentiation across financing
  stages (a company needs different financing at different stages,
  "the difficulty is it cannot build a one-size-fits-all") and should
  recognise that "success can be measured mainly by to those targets
  that are needed" rather than one static metric.
- **R&D tax credit changes in Budget 2027 flagged as genuinely
  welcome:** raising the first-year payment threshold from €87,500 to
  €105,000, and increasing the limits for qualifying R&D wage costs
  (recognising the importance of investing in skilled people).
  Extension of small-company R&D subcontracting limits to third-level
  institutions and Enterprise Ireland's Atlas Scaling Initiative also
  welcomed as helping companies access points of expertise across
  different sectors.
- **Start-Up Capital Incentive and Start-Up Relief for Entrepreneurs
  and Angel Investor Relief** — Budget 2027 extensions welcomed, but
  Shanahan argues these remain subject to EU state aid framework
  limits that constrain how much support younger businesses can
  actually access, and timing (getting capital to start-ups early
  enough) matters as much as the amount.
- **Headline corporation tax/capital gains tax changes:** extension of
  small-company relief, reduction of the standard capital gains tax
  rate for founders at the beginning of the journey from 33% to 31%
  (per article) — welcomed as a signal to founders and investors, but
  Shanahan's closing framing is that "none of these measures should be
  dismissed. Equally, none should be mistaken for a complete answer" —
  the real test is whether the capital, credit and extension of
  investment programme reaches companies without judging them
  consciously together.

**Cross-reference:** general Irish start-up/scale-up funding policy
context — relevant background for Meridian's own commercial
positioning (an Irish advisory business itself), and for Tairseach's
own funding environment given the ISIF/Enterprise Ireland institutional
capital angle discussed. No direct action item, but useful macro
context alongside the Enterprise Ireland AI Summit figures already
logged (Jenny Melia piece, yesterday's batch).

## 4. "As opposition to data centres grows, Ireland must take note" (Vincent Boland, The Big Picture column)

Strong, skeptical big-picture column — worth reading in full rather
than just summarising, given directness. Key points:

- **Global data-centre opposition is rising fast** — framed via a
  William Blake "dark satanic mills" analogy (the Industrial
  Revolution parallel). New-generation AI data centres are "gigantic,
  dark, windowless, almost devoid of human presence," consuming far
  more water and electricity than previous data-centre generations,
  raising real local utility-bill and environmental concerns.
- **US political dimension, directly relevant:** distrust of AI data
  centres is now a live political issue ahead of US mid-terms — polls
  show roughly half of American voters distrust AI data centres. Trump
  has criticised data-centre opponents, but some of his own Republican
  "fellow travellers" are running scared given the polling.
- **Ireland's "peculiar position," stated directly:** Ireland is a key
  European data-centre location — **108 live data centres today, with
  a further 30 at various stages of construction** (per Ronan Kelly,
  CEO of Digital Infrastructure Ireland). But these are mostly
  first/second-generation data centres — **no third-generation (AI-
  scale) data centres have yet been built here**, because a 2021 ban
  on connecting new data centres to the electricity grid (since lifted,
  but replaced by a conditions-heavy development framework) has made
  building the newest generation difficult here.
- **Two consequences flagged directly:**
  1. Ireland's existing data centres are **ageing rapidly relative to
     the technology** — "vital signs of the Irish tech industry are in
     the early stages of decay," per Kelly.
  2. **Irish data-centre engineering talent, unable to build at home
     under the moratorium, is now building AI data centres all over
     Europe instead** (Scandinavia, Spain) — a genuine skills/talent
     leakage risk directly tied to the local permitting environment.
- **Ownership/consolidation trend:** Irish data-centre developers and
  suppliers are being acquired at high prices — named example:
  **Kingspan buying Meath-based BMC Manufacturing** (a supplier to the
  data-centre industry) for up to **€900m.**
  
- **The sharper structural warning, worth John's attention given
  Tairseach's data-centre-land vertical:** "many of these new European
  data centres are being built for technology companies with their
  international headquarters in Ireland" — raising the prospect that,
  within a decade, those tech companies may funnel their new
  investment and **intellectual property away from Ireland** toward
  Finland, Spain and other European build locations — even though the
  "golden goose" (Irish-headquartered tax base) has delivered tens of
  billions in Exchequer taxes over the past decade. Boland's framing:
  "perhaps this will not happen, but it poses a medium-to-long-term
  threat to our economic development model... not yet confronted."
- **Boland's closing argument:** public resistance to AI data centres
  will only ease "when the world gets a bit of honesty and
  transparency from the tech industry" — data centres "are not wealth
  creators, except for their developers... what they consume — water,
  land, energy — are public goods." No AI without AI data centres, and
  no AI data centres without public acceptance — "this is Silicon
  Valley's dark satanic mills moment, and how it reacts could define
  the tech industry for a generation."

**Cross-reference:** directly, materially relevant to **Tairseach's
data-centre-land vertical** and the broader data-centre thread already
well tracked (`market_intel_datacentres_ireland_2026_conference_oct2026.md`,
`market_intel_data_centre_turning_point_sept2026.md`,
`market_intel_data_centre_jobs_ireland_oct2026.md`). This piece adds
a genuinely new and sharper angle not yet captured: the risk that
Ireland is stuck with ageing first/second-generation data centres
while third-generation AI-scale builds happen elsewhere in Europe
(built by Irish talent forced to work abroad), with a real risk of IP
and investment following those builds away from Ireland over time.
Worth flagging directly to Tairseach/Shane as a structural risk factor
in the data-centre-land thesis, not just a planning-delay inconvenience
as previously framed.
