## 1. "AI can be a valuable tool to help deliver infrastructure" (Shane MacSweeney, EY Global Infrastructure Leader)
Core argument: infrastructure delivery speed, not budget size, is now the
real competitive differentiator between countries — "the countries that
prosper over the next 20 years will not be those with the biggest
infrastructure budgets, but those that can deliver faster than
competitors." Cites the **EU's Critical Entities Resilience (CER)
Directive** as recognising transport, energy, water, telecoms and digital
platforms as strategic national assets that must stay operational through
climate shocks, cyber threats, geopolitical disruption and technological
change. Notes global investors now weigh energy security, digital
connectivity, transport efficiency, sustainability and resilience
alongside tax policy/labour availability when assessing a country.

**Central reframe, directly relevant to Meridian's positioning:** most
public debate treats AI as an infrastructure *challenge* (needing new data
centres, power, connectivity), but MacSweeney argues AI is also **part of
the solution** — a tool that can transform how infrastructure itself is
planned, designed, delivered and maintained, not just something that
consumes infrastructure capacity. Frames AI productivity gains as
potentially "the most valuable resource available" to governments facing
fiscal pressure and rising infrastructure demand. Also flags that as
infrastructure becomes more digitised/connected, it becomes more exposed
to cyberattack — "cyber resilience must be designed into this new
infrastructure from the outset, not as an afterthought." Closing point:
delivery speed itself is becoming a competitive advantage in its own
right.

## 2. "Buy European push spooks firms in EU and UK... fear it could deter foreign investment" (Sarah Collins)
The EU's proposed **Industrial Accelerator Act** — designed to boost
low-carbon manufacturing so EU firms can compete on a more even footing
with heavily subsidised Chinese rivals — would limit foreign-made
electric vehicles, solar panels, wind turbines, batteries and their
components/technology from receiving public money, preferring homegrown
EU firms for public tenders and state subsidies. While primarily targeted
at China, it's having a "chilling effect" on the US, UK, Switzerland,
Norway, Japan and Canada (a newer EU trade favourite).

**Ibec wrote to Enterprise Minister Peter Burke** ahead of a Brussels
meeting he chaired last week, warning the move could deter foreign
investment into Ireland specifically. **UK angle:** PM Andy Burnham and
Chancellor John Healey raised it directly with the Taoiseach and Tánaiste,
given the UK is trying to negotiate a wider "reset" with the EU and this
issue is complicating progress; London fears its car manufacturers could
be locked out of EU supply chains, with knock-on effects for Ireland
(which imports most of its used cars from Britain and Japan) — a British
Chamber of Commerce EU board member, **Fergus McReynolds**, warned "we
shouldn't be looking at things that put that integrated supply chain at
risk." The law is still being negotiated; unclear whether "partner
countries" with wide-ranging free trade deals (UK, Canada) will get
special tender access.

## 3. "Dear Vicki" — when to cut losses on a bad hire (Vicki O'Toole)
Advice column addressing a reader six months into a hire that "looked
perfect on paper" but isn't working out, despite early warning signs.
Key advice points, generally useful reference for any of John's ventures
managing staff/team decisions:
- Recruitment difficulty creates a bias toward denial — after investing
  weeks/months in hiring and onboarding, owners talk themselves out of
  early warning signs ("they're just settling in").
- **Distinguish a single bad day from a pattern**: one incident doesn't
  define a person, but repeated excuses for the same attitude/
  performance/reliability concerns means "you probably need to pause and
  look at what is actually happening, rather than what you hoped would
  happen."
- "Giving someone a chance" should not mean an open-ended chance — if
  concerns exist, have a specific conversation about what isn't working,
  what needs to change, and by when, then honestly assess whether there's
  real improvement or just moved goalposts.
- **Team and reputational cost of inaction**: good staff notice when poor
  performance is tolerated selectively, which breeds frustration;
  customers experiencing a bad hire "don't know you made a bad hire, they
  simply see your business."
- Closing line, worth quoting: "there is no prize for hanging on to the
  wrong person for two years instead of six months... don't let the
  difficulty of finding the next person be the reason you keep the wrong
  one."
