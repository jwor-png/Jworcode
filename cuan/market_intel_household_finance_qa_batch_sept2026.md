## 1. "Irregular expenses catch me by surprise" — budgeting advice
Reader question (Deirdre, Co. Roscommon): manages regular monthly
budget well but is caught out by irregular costs (Christmas, birthdays,
car insurance, back-to-school, car/home repairs). Advice given:
- Distinguish genuine emergencies (job loss, major car repair, sudden
  loss of income — needs a dedicated emergency fund, ideally covering
  essential expenses for a period) from **predictable-but-irregular**
  costs (Christmas, birthdays, back-to-school, annual insurance) which
  should be planned for in advance, not treated as emergencies.
- Practical method: look back over the last 12 months, write down all
  non-monthly expenses faced, add them up, divide the total by 12 —
  that monthly amount should be set aside proactively (e.g. a separate
  "sinking fund" savings pot) so those larger bills don't disrupt the
  regular budget when they land. Example cited: irregular costs
  averaging ~€3,600/year work out to ~€300/month set aside gradually.
- Emergency funds and irregular-expense funds should be kept separate,
  since they serve different purposes (unplanned emergencies vs known
  future costs).

## 2. "How to use AI to plan your shop and slash your food bills" (Arlene Harris) — full feature
**Orla Cusack**, described as an "accidental content creator" with
575,000+ followers across social media (her "Practical and Precious"
pages), is a former Garda who retired and became a one-income family,
prompting her to overhaul household grocery spending. Background:
always fairly mindful of money, became "much more conscious of how and
where our money was going" after the 2008 crash; the real shift came on
retiring from the Gardaí and needing to "cut our cloth to suit our
measure." Became far more organised — checking fridge/freezer/cupboard
stock before shopping, planning meals around what's already at home,
paying closer attention to what's genuinely needed rather than being
led by supermarket "special offers, displays and product placement."
Weekly grocery bill fell from €200-250 to a more controlled figure
through habit change, not a single trick — "the real difference came
from developing better habits and repeating them every week."

She started using ChatGPT for grocery help about a year ago: "I was
already shopping carefully and had my system, but I found AI useful
when I was tired, short on time or stuck for ideas" — explicitly
framing **AI as a helping hand rather than something to replace my
judgement** — you can tell it your budget, how many people you're
feeding, where you shop, what your family will or won't eat, and what
you already have at home, and it will suggest meals using ingredients
across several days or create a shopping list; you can even show it a
photo of your fridge contents and ask for ideas to prevent food waste.

She has since created a paid digital guide, **"The Savvy Shopping
Guide,"** built around: a "fridge-first" approach, household food
inventories, weekly planning, meal planning, spending pauses, backup
meals, reset weeks, and practical AI prompts — explicitly designed as
"a realistic system which ordinary households could adapt to suit
their own budget and circumstances... not about perfection, and it
certainly isn't about telling people never to buy anything nice."
Cusack's own guide-book pricing (cited elsewhere in the piece) fell
from €200-250 to around €150 over the past year, and she estimates
households following the system could save €50-100/week (up to
~€5,000/year).

**Printed "Top tips for reducing costs" checklist:** check fridge/
freezer/cupboards before writing a list and shop from what you already
have; plan meals around ingredients already at home; decide your budget
before entering the supermarket; write a clear list and stick to it;
compare price-per-unit/per-kilogram rather than assuming the largest
pack or a special offer is automatically best value; keep simple
backup meals at home for busy evenings to avoid unplanned takeaways;
be realistic about what your family will actually eat, since food is
not good value if it ends up in the bin; have an occasional "reset
week" using up what's already in the house; don't remove every treat,
since the system needs to be realistic/sustainable to stick.

**CSO/pricing context in the same feature:** a typical household
grocery shop now costs ~25% more than five years ago; beef up 32-50%;
cheddar up 57c/kg, milk up 27c/litre, butter up to 42c/kg; even small
shopping lists can produce unexpectedly high bills, forcing some
households to choose between going without or "kicking the can down
the road" onto credit cards.

## 3. "I sold my car and left a job in finance to become a stand-up comedian" — My Money, Darren Gaffney
**Darren Gaffney**, from rural Co. Cavan, left a finance career to
pursue comedy full-time; runs the "Bite the Bullet" comedy clubs
(Dublin and Cavan), TikTok sketches with 2.1m+ likes; performed at
Electric Picnic's Comedy Arena, The Sugar Club and Whelan's (Dublin),
the Paddy Power Comedy Festival, with two shows at Galway Comedy
Festival on 20 October (galwaycomedyfestival.ie). Money-attitude
highlights: parents encouraged him to earn his own money early
(worked on an uncle's farm), taught him to value money and be sensible
with earnings; worst job was working as a binman aged 16; biggest
financial regret framed as a joke about a round of pints in Temple Bar,
more seriously flags cars as "a luxury and a financial drain" and that
people worry too much about appearances, which "can get them into
trouble." Biggest extravagance: bought a 2021 Ford Focus ST-Line while
still working in finance, sold it when he quit for comedy, citing
*Rich Dad Poor Dad*'s framing that "the wealthy buy assets first while
others buy liabilities first, and that wealth is 'quiet' and broke is
'flashy'" — buy assets first, worry about lifestyle later. Rents in
Dublin, says the housing ladder "won't be as easy for me as it was for
my parents." Main money-saving hack: "invest it... credit union and
savings accounts are a waste of time [for young people]. Ask for advice
and invest in stocks or a mutual fund." Has a pension only from his old
finance job; none from comedy, so plans to "generate a lot of cash" to
compensate. Three non-negotiable spends: "Barry's tea, steak, and a
proper carbonara in an Italian restaurant."

## 4. "Should I use most of our savings to pay off our €7,000 loan?" — Core Credit Union
**Michael Byrne, CEO, Core Credit Union.** Reader (Aoife, Co. Wexford)
question: has €9,000 in savings, ~€7,000 outstanding on a personal
loan, two young children, wants to know whether to use most savings to
clear the loan or keep saving alongside continuing repayments. Byrne's
advice: clearing the loan is appealing (especially if not debt-free
otherwise) but with young children an emergency fund matters more —
recommends keeping a relatively small buffer (~€2,000) accessible for
genuine unexpected costs, and using perhaps €3,500 toward the loan
(reducing outstanding balance to ~€3,500, leaving ~€5,500 in savings)
rather than clearing it entirely in one go. Notes: check whether the
loan agreement charges any early-repayment fee/penalty before making a
lump-sum repayment; consider running the numbers with the lender/credit
union directly to compare the interest saved against the value of
keeping a larger accessible buffer, since future circumstances/ability
to repay could change.
