# "How to meet Burnham's ambitions on social care" — Camilla Cavendish, FT Weekend Opinion

Source: FT Weekend, Camilla Cavendish (politics columnist). VERIFIED — named FT columnist's opinion piece, UK-focused but directly comparable to the eldercare/ageing-population thesis already tracked for Ireland.

## What it says

The UK prime minister's (Andy Burnham, per the article — a 2026 Labour leadership context) first big speech in the job felt more like an election manifesto than an address from the midterm leader of a cash-strapped country. Cavendish is skeptical of some of his broader political positioning (nationalisation, municipal socialism, electoral reform to woo the Liberal Democrats) but credits him on social care specifically: he is "absolutely right to break the supposedly untouchable triple lock on pensions and link it to social care."

**Why social care matters more than it looks:** it "sounds like a sideshow, but is increasingly central in countries with ageing populations." In England, up to a third of hospital beds at any one time are occupied by medically fit people who can't go home without support — meanwhile the elderly person loses fitness lying flat in hospital, ending up with worse mobility. This is a direct structural echo of Tadhg Daly's ageing-dialogue op-ed already logged (`market_intel_tadhg_daly_ageing_dialogue_oct2026.md`) — bed/capacity pressure driven by inadequate step-down/home-care support, not just raw demand.

**Burnham's approach — could go either way:** "could either bankrupt the country or usher in a better old age." He appears to want to copy the Scottish model (some free services), but Cavendish argues if he can curb his statist instincts and learn from what works elsewhere, he might fashion something genuinely innovative, directing money to staff rather than to complexity and compliance.

**Why previous attempts to "fix" social care have failed, per Cavendish — three reasons:**
1. Many voters still think social care is free, just like healthcare (Theresa May lost her Conservative majority in 2017 partly for leveling with the public on this).
2. Many resist the idea of having to sell their home to pay for care, expecting other taxpayers to foot the bill — but no system is sustainable without some call on the main asset.
3. The Treasury turns down proposals to spend more without any commensurate improvement in outcomes.

**The current system described as "an inhumane, bureaucratic mess":** people are reduced to units on a spreadsheet by local authority contracts; agencies bid to provide home visits sometimes as little as 15 minutes; people open their door to a revolving cast of strangers; council social workers must do a new assessment even for tiny changes to a "care package"; the workforce is valiant and demoralised, made to clock in even before saying "hello" by public commissioners who don't trust independent providers.

**Care workers "invisible to the political class," per Burnham, and to the NHS, which looks down on them.** Cavendish's own grimmest pandemic-era experience: chairing a meeting with very vulnerable people needing help, when some GPs and councils weren't even answering the phone — one man in a wheelchair had been saved by two Polish care workers who simply decided to move in with him. As a temporary adviser to the Department of Health at the time, she saw care workers stepping up to manage wounds, give insulin and vaccinations — tasks they're not usually allowed to do — yet they still get little recognition.

**What Burnham should actually pursue, per Cavendish:** not an "NHS-style social care system" but something closer to the insurance-based systems of Germany and Japan, which are sustainable and highly popular, built on "everyone contributes and everyone is covered." She hopes he'll let Louise Casey's review (reporting next summer) look at those models specifically.

**The conversation always starts with funding and gets stuck there — but what's as important is a radical change in culture and systems.** Good social care is about building relationships. In parts of the Netherlands, Japan and even the UK, much better care is achieved where frontline staff have autonomy to decide what individuals need, helping maintain independence by "doing with" not "doing to." A worker who can decide to take Mrs Jones to a lunch club because she's lonely, or pop back to see Mrs Smith who was tearful that morning, is much more likely to stay in the job. This outcomes-focused approach also involves staff having support networks outside the rigid boundaries of the state, with their clients' neighbours, friends and relatives.

**Sadly the NHS "can't compute autonomy"** — attempts to introduce the Dutch model (Buurtzorg-style self-managing teams) in parts of England foundered against bureaucracies that resisted jettisoning forms or letting teams manage themselves. The idea that the NHS should "take over" social care overlooks the ecosystem of charities, social enterprises and independent providers already doing this work. When these work well with councils it means an end to duplication and a big reduction in the amount of data requested, hence real savings — in Wigan, this approach has improved patient satisfaction while reducing pressures across all public services.

**On funding:** changing the pensions triple lock won't fund a new social care model on its own, not least because the savings in any one year will change depending on the relationship between earnings and inflation — but it will end the "built-in ratchet effect," which is badly needed. Politically astute framing: suggesting money from pensioners will be returned to pensioners, just in a different way. Cavendish's closing line: "at the very least, Burnham deserves credit for breaking the omertà on that."
