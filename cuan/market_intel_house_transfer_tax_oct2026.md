# "We gave our house to our children. Have we accidentally created a tax nightmare?", Mary Frances Fahy (solicitor and tax consultant, Fahy Neilan Solicitors), Irish Independent Farming p.9, Tue 13 Oct 2026 (John's photo, 17 Nov 2026). Legal advice column; figures and rules as reported, UNVERIFIED, not advice for John.

**The question:** parents signed their house over to their four children "a few years ago", for tax reasons; no right of residence was registered on the folio (only set out in a letter to the children's solicitors); they still live there rent-free, utilities in their names. Worries: is living there a taxable gift from the children (gift or inheritance tax), what happens on a sale (capital gains), should they pay rent, and did they do the right thing.

**The answer (as reported):**
- She would never advise transferring the family home to children while the owners are alive; the transfer was "badly advised" and "a can of worms for all sorts of tax issues".
- No registered right of residence means an informal arrangement: the parents are vulnerable if there is a falling out, a child dies or is in a relationship breakdown (in-laws), and the children could make life difficult.
- Gift or inheritance tax (CAT): parents are in group B with a lifetime threshold of 40,000 euro each; gifts or inheritances from uncles, aunts, brothers or sisters reduce it; each parent can take 3,000 euro a year tax-free from each of four children (12,000 euro each); the rent of a room and shared services is unlikely to exceed that, so gift tax is unlikely; if a right of residence is registered, it is a potentially taxable benefit valued by a formula (age and gender based; the older the person, the lower the value).
- Capital gains tax: the house is not the children's principal private residence, so they face a possible liability on the uplift from the transfer date to sale; possible "dependent relative relief" only if the parent is incapacitated by old age or infirmity, cannot maintain themselves, occupies the house rent-free as sole residence, and no consideration is given; not if the parents have pensions or rental income. Renting to the children would lose that relief.
- "Strictly from a tax point of view, you might be better off leaving matters as they are", but not safe without a registered right of residence.
