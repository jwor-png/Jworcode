# "Students hire lawyers to fight AI misconduct accusations from universities" — Josh Gabert-Doyon

Source: Financial Times, Higher Education section. VERIFIED mainstream press report citing named lawyers, a named law firm, Universities UK, and a specific 2024 academic study.

## What it says

**Scale of the problem:** Universities UK (the industry group representing higher education institutions) said the total number of student disciplinary cases flagged for using AI in graded coursework is difficult to establish, although a 2024 survey of 20 public universities found more than 95,000 students at those institutions faced some form of internal disciplinary process related to formal misconduct — and about 9% of US students admitted to using AI tools to cheat.

**Barrister's account — Daniel Sokol KC, 12 King's Bench Walk:** his firm received 300 requests related to allegations of AI misconduct in the first half of this year — roughly 40% of his firm's total caseload. He describes a "tsunami" of student inquiries from those facing AI misconduct accusations, with "the biggest tragedies" being innocent students being found guilty. Legal action specialist Ciaran Donaghy (Quality Assurance Agency, a UK higher education policy body) noted that many universities' policies on AI use integrate individual departments or institutions, varying widely and deviating from what some lecturers individually draw the line on — unclear guidance about what constitutes AI misconduct, with some universities reliant on AI-detection tools that Sokol described as "flawed." The Quality Assurance Agency has responded by softening the threshold of what constitutes AI misconduct, using AI alongside softer academic-practice standards, as the term "poor academic practice" becomes increasingly used to distinguish certain forms of AI use.

**Specific case — "Alice," a King's College London music degree student (name changed):** submitted an essay for her degree in May; told by the university it "wasn't the best thing" compared with her earlier coursework, and was investigated for AI misconduct. Alice shared her notes and early drafts of the essay with university administrators, but was still told she would be questioned by a faculty misconduct panel. She described the experience as an "emotionally intense" ending to her degree. Despite sharing the full development history of the coursework, she was not able to immediately resolve the accusation internally — she sought legal advice because the university's own student support services were too busy, and panicked that she would not complete her degree and waste tens of thousands of pounds in tuition fees.

**Internal AI misconduct case — Louise O'Driscoll's solicitor, representing "Alice" (a different individual, student at an unnamed university):** submitted an essay for her final year, and was told by the university it "wasn't the best thing" she had ever written. She was surprised to receive an email from her university acknowledging that AI usage had "notable increase" in similar cases this year, accusing her of having used AI to write coursework. However, she was acknowledged to have deviated from her own real name — described as internal coursework, "that probably goes a lot more than it probably comes to light."

**Threshold and academic-integrity confusion:** when it comes to AI usage, students must learn to use it honestly and well, the group added. Some universities have responded by escalating internal disciplinary cases or formal legal action; Sokol said he's seen the threshold of what constitutes misconduct shift over the course of the year as universities update AI-use policies, with some lecturers and departments holding stricter standards than others — creating inconsistent, hard-to-navigate rules for students across a single institution.

**Named firms/individuals developing a specialism:** Daniel Sokol (12 King's Bench Walk, barrister); Ciaran Donaghy (Quality Assurance Agency); a Bath and Queen Mary University London study on AI-detection tool reliability (concerns about false-positive accusations); international/ESL students flagged as being at higher risk of being falsely accused by AI-detection software, per a 2024 Stanford University study, which found AI-detection tools were more likely to flag non-native English speakers' writing as AI-generated due to differences in vocabulary/sentence structure patterns.

**Student quote (unnamed, a university student caught using AI without authorisation, describing her own genuine but unauthorised use):** "I use AI day-to-day as a personal assistant," she said. "If you are at a university right now, you are going to be at higher risk of being falsely accused — and she offered a simple explanation for why she felt her university-aged students were going to pick up the way that chatbots talk."
