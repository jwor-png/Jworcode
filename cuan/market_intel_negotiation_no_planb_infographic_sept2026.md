## The seven points
1. **Don't let your BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)
   set your target.** A weak BATNA should change your strategy, not your
   goal — keep the target fixed and adjust the approach, rather than
   lowering ambition because your fallback option is weak.
2. **Manage your weakness deliberately.** Be intentional about what you
   reveal (goals, priorities, relevant context — builds credibility),
   what you manage/share selectively (constraints, budget range,
   flexibility, timing, internal approval), and what you protect/never
   reveal (lack of alternatives, true deadline, internal pressure,
   desperation, walk-away limit, what you genuinely can't accept).
3. **Build a BATNA for your BATNA.** If you have no strong alternative,
   build leverage elsewhere: buy time (extend the deadline), reduce
   dependence (solve part of the problem elsewhere), create options
   (find another supplier/buyer/partner/path), build leverage (now
   negotiate from strength) — you don't need a perfect BATNA, just
   options that make you less dependent on one outcome.
4. **Change the negotiation metric if you can't win on price.** Add more
   variables to trade beyond price alone (volume, timing, scope, payment
   terms, risk, guarantees, commitment). Core principle: **every
   concession buys something — don't concede, trade** (e.g. give a lower
   price, get higher volume; give faster delivery, get longer
   commitment; give more scope, get better payment terms).
5. **Add new players (3-D negotiation)** — if you can't create leverage
   alone, change who's in the game: add a competitor (creates
   competition), add a partner (shares the problem), add an ally
   (increases influence), or find a new table (take the decision to a
   different venue/decision-maker).
6. **Negotiate interests, not positions** (credited to Harvard). Go
   beyond what's literally asked for and understand what the other side
   actually cares about — a stated position ("lower the price") usually
   masks an underlying interest (reduce budget risk, get internal
   approval), which opens room for a trade that solves their real need
   (e.g. keep the price, reduce upfront payment) — common underlying
   interests cited: lower risk, predictable costs, faster implementation,
   internal approval, long-term relationship, better results, less
   effort, strategic advantage, certainty.
7. **Build the "grand slam offer"** (credited to Alex Hormozi) — rather
   than negotiating harder, build an offer difficult to refuse: bigger
   outcome (more of what they actually want), higher certainty
   (guarantees, proof, references), faster result (shorter timeline,
   quick wins, fewer delays), less effort required from them (you handle
   the work, simplify the process) — the combination makes it "hard to
   refuse."
