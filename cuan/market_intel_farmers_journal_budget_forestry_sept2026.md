# Irish Farmers Journal batch: Budget 2027 farm schemes and forestry features

Summaries paraphrased from the print articles. REPORTED/VERIFIED mainstream agricultural trade press, figures as stated in each piece.

## 1. "Budget 2027: schemes competing for funding" (Darren Carty, Agribusiness)

Budget 2027 due 6 October, against a backdrop of escalating input costs, mediocre output prices and lingering drought effects — mounting calls for greater support funding.

- **Fertiliser support:** co-funding under the EU Fertiliser Action Plan is the hot topic. Ireland's share of the €540m fund is €15.3m; Government could implement the maximum national co-funding top-up of 200%. Minister Heydon seeking cabinet support for a fund worth up to €45.9m (€11/t for each tonne purchased in 2025, up to €33/t if maximum funding allocated). Discussions ongoing on reallocating unspent fuel-support-scheme funds to this pot.
- **Tillage support:** tillage/horticulture received €32.36m (2025) and €30m (2026) support, packaged into an overall €50m sector support package (Protein Aid Scheme + Straw Incorporation Measure). Tillage editor Siobhán Walsh reported €34.4m spent on SIM in the first three years, potentially up to €15m to fund the scheme in 2026 — additional funding likely required in 2027; hoped at least €50m allocated to cover SIM and a Tillage Support Scheme for cash-strapped growers.
- **Animal health:** €10m delivered in recent years, utilised by TASAH and a bovine viral diarrhoea tissue-tag support payment. No real changes expected in the upcoming budget.
- **Organics scheme:** funding has grown steadily with participation; new applicant growth has slowed but appetite remains to convert more tillage/dairy farmers to organic. A limited increase is expected, unlikely to match last year's jump.
- **Dairy beef schemes:** National Dairy Beef Weighing Scheme returned 2025 after strong calls for reintroduction; €4m budget heavily oversubscribed, eligible calves for the €20/head payment reduced from 50 to 31 calves. Could remain unchanged around €4m.
- **Beef support:** National Beef Welfare Scheme allocated €28m in Budget 2026 — insufficient to cover a payment-rate increase from €50 to €75/calf plus an eligible-calf increase from 40 to 45. Rate subsequently cut to €67/calf; scheme likely to remain oversubscribed with linear cuts applying; funding expected to remain €28m in Budget 2027.
- **ACRES funding:** likely higher allocation again in 2027 to meet existing commitments; some budget pressure for particular years likely to remain unchanged; a significant number of farmers paid in January 2026, suggesting the scheme dips into the next year's budget to cover payments.
- **TB action plan:** Budget 2026's big surprise was an €85m funding increase for the bovine TB programme, bringing total funds to €157m. TB reactor numbers for H1 2026 down 20% (43,290 to 34,886) after increasing 70% over the previous two years — quiet expectation this funding level continues to year-end, freeing scope elsewhere if savings aren't needed in line with the 20% reactor reduction.
- **Sheep support:** National Sheep Welfare Scheme funding in Budget 2026 initially looked reduced by €2m but was revised to the prior year's €22m figure; per-ewe payment cut from €13 to €11.50 due to oversubscription. Farm organisations flag linear cuts as a "red line" issue; similar treatment expected for 2027.
- **Generational renewal:** Taoiseach Micheál Martin, Tánaiste Simon Harris and Minister Martin Heydon all emphasised generational renewal importance at the Ploughing. Harris floated tax-incentive prospects; Heydon confirmed no intention to alter current inheritance policies; possible incentives for succession or farm partnerships.
- **Miscellaneous:** €2.5m allocated in recent years for farm safety; red clover silage/multispecies swards measures likely to remain absent from TAMS allowances.

## 2. "Lameness: tackling the performance killer" (vet Q&A)

Lameness affecting >5% of a flock/herd is a fixable problem; successfully tackling it yields multiples back in time/effort/cost. Peak lameness season for sheep and dairy herds now (mating prep, softer grass, foot scald progressing to footrot). Five-point plan and footbathing remain the tried-and-trusted methods to keep incidence below 5%; digital dermatitis in cows often needs an easing of footbathing protocols; drought-related condition loss reduces digital fat pad cushioning, raising ulcer risk.

## 3. "RDS announces the 40th anniversary of Forestry Awards" (Donal Magner)

RDS Forest and Woodland Awards mark 40 years in 2027 (tradition traces to 1741 Dublin Society timber-crop premiums; revived as an awards scheme in 1987). €16,000 prize fund for winners/runners-up, plus RDS Silver Medal and trophies. Four 2027 award categories: Silvicultural Excellence and Innovation; Community Woodland; Teagasc Farm Forestry; Native Woodland Establishment (5-30 years old). Open to all forests/plantations regardless of size. Entries close 9 October 2026; winners announced next April at the RDS Agriculture and Forestry Awards Ceremony.

Also: Irish Agroforestry Forum hosting a Farm Walk series in Northern Ireland, 5-8 October, supported by the Shared Island Fund — exploring integrating trees onto productive farms for water quality, flood mitigation and farm resilience in the Lough Neagh catchment.

## 4. "Forestry provides a continuous income" (Donal Magner, interview with James Bennett)

James Bennett, Laois forest owner since 1989, built 26ha of forestry on land unsuitable for conventional farming (mainly Sitka spruce, Norway spruce at higher elevations). Phased planting produced continuous income via State premiums, thinning and final harvest — younger plantations attracted premiums of €746/ha even during a clearfell of an older forest property that yielded over €100,000 after replanting costs. Attributes profitability to early and continuous thinning; average annual income worked out at €750/ha on the clearfell. An outright RDS Farm Forestry Award winner in 2005, he urges other forest owners to enter the awards and shares his knowledge. His message: "Forestry provides continuous income as well as being an ideal pension fund on marginal land, but you need a minimum of 10ha and ideally more than 20ha." Notes restrictions on planting unenclosed land, shallow peat and hen-harrier-designated areas need reassessing for forestry to become a serious business model on family farms.

## 5. "Reliable prices are vital for forestry owners" (Donal Whelan, ITGA technical director)

CSO figures show 57% of 2025 roundwood removals came from privately-owned forests — informed, confident growers need accurate, independently compiled price data. The Wood Price Quarterly (WPQ), developed with UCD (Assistant Professor Amanda Sosa, School of Agriculture and Food Science) since 2015, independently and confidentially compiles standing roundwood prices from growers, consultant foresters, forestry companies; only compiled averages are published, individual submissions stay confidential. Results freely available via the ITGA website and the Association's Forestry & Timber Yearbook; roundwood sales data forms available by emailing roundwood-database@ucd.ie.

## 6. "Maximising the long-term value of your forest" / windblow coverage (Talking Timber event, Charleville Park Hotel)

Teagasc's 15th year of the Talking Timber advisory event. Key points: site conditions/species choice/timely management affect productivity and resilience (Dr Niall Farrelly); timber quality presentation by Seán Kelly (Glennon Brothers Sawmills); DAFM forestry inspector Ciarán Nugent outlined supports for thinning/clearfelling including the Forest Road Scheme and Innovative Forest Technology Scheme (machinery/equipment modernisation funding); Kathleen Lucey (Irish Forest Owners) on forest certification and the DAFM Forest iPLAN scheme — certification increasingly important as private timber supply increases and processors seek independently-verified, sustainably managed timber; group certification can share audit costs via centralised support.

**Windblow challenge:** an estimated one million m³ blown down in Northern Ireland (storm Éowyn) adds to an already acute windblow problem, with no cross-border initiative to address it (unlike the ash dieback response). Record private timber harvesting in 2025 (2.95m m³ per CSO) likely mostly windblown, as owners with standing crops are reluctant to sell into a log-saturated market. Forestry companies expect to be harvesting windblow until at least the end of next year. Windblown forests in the 25-30 age category (previously thinned, most attractive) have sawlog prices fallen to €40/m³ — still a reasonable price given the Reconstitution Scheme for Windblow (RSW) will cover most reforestation costs. Younger plantations are harder to sell given oversupply; pulpwood prices fallen from €14/m³ to as low as €7/m³ according to some foresters. RSW take-up still low as foresters/companies focus on harvesting first. RSW available for forests with ≥20% damage; grants range €3,858-€6,744/ha by forest type (highest for native/broadleaf-oak-beech forests, lowest for diverse conifer).

## 7. "Why forestry is missing from farm viability debate" (Donal Magner, Focus: Forestry introduction)

Agricultural land-use viability is assessed by Teagasc for five main farm types (dairy, cattle rearing, cattle other, sheep, tillage) — 135,000 farms, 4.5m ha total. Apart from dairy, forestry can compete with all land types as a revenue generator, yet doesn't feature in the annual Teagasc National Farm Survey. Farmers own roughly 80% of Ireland's 380,000ha of private forests (7% of the agricultural land bank — the same area as all tillage farms combined, three times the size of mixed livestock farms), and the sector's total annual economic contribution (State and private) is estimated by COFORD at over €2bn. Reason for exclusion: survey confined to average farm sizes in the 30ha (cattle)/50ha (sheep)/70ha (dairy/tillage) range; farms under 15ha excluded. Average new-forest planting size has fallen from 12ha (early 1990s) to just 5ha in recent years, so forestry doesn't qualify as a business model at that scale. Argument: incentivising forests of commercial scale similar to viable beef systems (30-35ha) could yield up to €30,000/year tax-free income, making forestry a genuine business model on marginal land without reducing agricultural production.

## 8. "Strong case for commercial forestry in Ireland" (Donal Magner)

Irish forests yield more than three times higher than Scandinavian forests (Austria, Sweden, Finland regard farm forestry as essential farm land use), strengthening the case for commercial Irish forestry. Teagasc surveys show average forestry Annual Equivalent Values (AEVs) can equal/outperform sheep, tillage and most cattle systems — up to €710/ha/year for Forest Type 12 (mainly Sitka spruce) — though these surveys exclude small forests (<15ha) and don't reflect that average forest size has fallen from 5ha to 3.25ha over 30 years, well below what produces such returns. Niall Farrelly and the late Gerhardt Gallagher identified 490,000ha of marginal land that could be afforested without negatively affecting agricultural output — but a mandatory Environmental Impact Assessment threshold reduction (from 200ha to 70ha in 1996, further to 50-70ha per 2026 DAFM statistics, plus sub-threshold EIAs for "afforestation likely to have a significant effect on the environment") militates against forestry at scale. The mid-term review needs to examine commercial forests' role not just in a viable forestry/forest-products sector but in meeting Ireland's Climate Action Plan targets, alongside the existing condition of setting aside 35% of planted areas for broadleaves/open biodiverse understorey.

Native Tree Area schemes: dry mineral sites €6,744 grant/€2,206 premium; wet mineral sites €6,744/€2,284. Premium duration: 15 years (non-farmers) or 20 years (farmers) for most categories. Additional fencing grants up to €1,140/ha (stock/sheep/rabbit), new deer fencing capped at €3,800/ha.

## 9. "Forestry as a sustainable farm business model" (Niall Collins TD, Minister of State with responsibility for forestry)

Ministerial opinion piece: Ireland's maritime climate/fertile soils produce timber yields well above the European average, making forestry an attractive diversification alongside carbon storage, renewable building materials and rural employment benefits. Current forestry programme provides income-tax-free annual premiums up to €1,142/ha for 20 years, while farmers retain Basic Income Support for Sustainability (BISS) on eligible afforested lands. Trees continue growing through economic downturns, increasing timber volume available for future sale — a longer-term investment potential that is why pension funds regularly invest in forestry; particularly valuable as farmers wind down or plan retirement, since the forest itself remains an asset passable to the next generation. Encourages visiting gov.ie/forestry for farmer case studies and practical next-step information.

## 10. "Developer questions the urgency on AD biomethane rollout" (Stephen Robb, Renewables)

Irish anaerobic digestion developer Carbon AMS has challenged Government delays to the Renewable Heat Obligation (RHO), asking "where is the urgency?" — following a Climate Change Advisory Council warning that slow progress is undermining investor confidence in indigenous biomethane development. Under the National Biomethane Strategy, Government committed to 5.7 TWh of indigenous biomethane production by 2030; latest EPA projections estimate production will reach just 1.75 TWh — under one-third of target, a projected shortfall of 3.95 TWh. RHO timeline has slipped nearly four years (first announced Nov 2022, General Scheme approved July 2025, bill remained unpublished as of September 2026, now not expected before 2027). A proposed domestic-production multiplier was found incompatible with EU single-market rules by the European Commission (March 2026) and has been removed. SEAI National Energy Balance: just 39.3 GWh of indigenously produced biomethane was injected to the gas grid 2022-2024 — equivalent to 0.7% of the 2030 target. Carbon AMS CEO Richard Kennedy: "What the industry needs now is certainty" — cites Ireland importing ~4/5 of its energy, being the only European country with no gas storage, and prices set by markets Ireland doesn't control. Carbon AMS is currently building a large-scale AD plant in Duleek, Co. Meath, with an €80m/15-year biomethane supply agreement with Alexion/AstraZeneca Rare Disease; first supply required Q4 2026.

## 11. "...at Roscommon farm walk" (TAMS III / Farm Safety Capital Investment Scheme coverage)

Farm walk on Patrick Rogers' farm, Castleplunkett, Co. Roscommon, covering practical TAMS compliance points: **Card A** — a minimum 5 working days' notice must be given to the Department before pouring concrete for a silo/mass concrete tank, to allow inspection; failure is an automatic 5% penalty; if no Department contact within 5 days, work can proceed. **Internal agitation points** — an older shed requiring slat replacement had no external agitation points, limiting grant aid to extending the tank for external points; shared air space between sheds with internal agitation points can render an otherwise-eligible shed ineligible — options are creating external agitation points in the adjoining shed, or installing side cladding (which could adversely affect ventilation). **Ranking and selection (TAMS III, tranche 12 cut-off marks)** — highest cut-off marks exist for the lowest-approval-rate schemes (Dairy Equipment Scheme, Solar Capital Investment Scheme); Organic Capital Investment Scheme also has a high cut-off, with organic farmers mainly gaining marks via ANC land volume (2.5 marks for 5-10ha, 5 marks for 10-20ha, 7.5 marks for 20-30ha, 10 marks for 30ha+); any scheme requiring nutrient storage grants 40 marks immediately, meaning all eligible dung-store/slurry-storage applications are approved.

## 12. "...in applying for TAMS highlighted" (continuation of the Roscommon farm walk, Seamus Nolan/Teagasc)

Further TAMS compliance detail: **which bank account pays matters** — an investment paid out of the wrong account (e.g. a joint account, or an account not matching who's actually applying) can be deemed 100% ineligible; "if you pay for an investment out of a joint bank account, that will be deemed 100% ineligible." Investments under a Registered Farm Partnership (RFP) or company must be paid from the RFP/company account. **Cash payments are non-compliant** — a full paper trail is required for all investments including deposits; "while cash might be king, it's not the king when it comes to TAMS." **Machinery purchases** must go through the applicant's own account, not a supplier's or a third party's. **Contractor tax compliance** — builders must hold an up-to-date tax clearance certificate for the farmer to be paid out their grant; most are registered Department customers so this shouldn't need chasing, but a missing/expired cert means the Department comes back to the farmer. **All payments must be completed and receipted before submission** — no outstanding balances on invoices. **Timeline** — 12 months from approval to complete building work/install mobile items; one 6-month extension available if the applicant shows an effort has been made on construction. For a slatted-tank element specifically, a deposit must be paid and receipted as evidence of that effort. **Inspections** — 5-10% of investments inspected pre-approval; a shed built to different dimensions than the application (e.g. claimed 30ft, measured 29ft) is treated as an overclaim; farmers can be inspected for up to 5 years from date of **payment** (not date of application) to confirm the building remains in their control — relevant for anyone considering leaving farming/leasing out the farm, which can trigger clawback. Species-specific investments (e.g. sheep sheds) require that enterprise to remain on-farm for 5 years post-payment; mobile investments (e.g. a bale splitter) must remain on-farm the same 5 years, though a like-for-like trade-in is allowed (no further grant aid on the replacement). **TAMS III tranche 12 cut-off marks (% selection):** AWNSS 75%, DES (Dairy Equipment Scheme) 20%, FSCIS (Farm Safety Capital Investment Scheme) 100%, LESS 10%, OCIS (Organic Capital Investment Scheme) 75%, PPIS 75%, SCIS (Solar Capital Investment Scheme) 10%, TCIS 70%, WFCIS 75%.

## 13. "6,000 nuts and bolts, 400,000 gallons... but does building a slurry tower pay off?" (Siobhán Walsh)

Andy O'Donnell and his family (Gowran, Co. Kilkenny) purchased a secondhand slurry tower via Facebook Marketplace, deconstructed and rebuilt it on their tillage farm (6,000 nuts and bolts, countless hours) to store slurry, aiming to maximise use of organic manures. The 400,000-gallon tower allows slurry deliveries/spreading year-round including in wet weather, avoiding the farmer being under pressure to spread within a narrow window; mixing in the tower gives a consistent product with known dry matter/nutrient content. Slurry tests showed N-P-K content of 15-1-21 per 1,000 gallons (equivalent to a 50kg bag of artificial fertiliser); applying in spring captures ~50% of available nitrogen immediately, valued at ~€35/1,000 gallons, with spreading cost estimated at €10/1,000 gallons — a net benefit of ~€25/1,000 gallons. O'Donnell expects the tower to pay for itself in 3-4 years, after which it could save ~€10,000/year at current fertiliser prices. Separately, Teagasc's Soil Cycle project (Ciarán Collins, a European Innovation Partnership project running to 2029) is trialling organic manure use on winter cereals; adviser Sean Óg McCormack found logistical inefficiencies (e.g. downtime at spreading) added over €60/ha to slurry-spreading costs in 2026 — a nurse tank helped reduce this. Cost comparison for fertilising winter wheat (40km round trip, 6,000-gallon tankers): slurry + artificial fertiliser came to €588/ha (four tankers) or €527/ha (five tankers + nurse tank), versus €530/ha for artificial fertiliser alone — showing a nurse tank and sufficient tanker numbers can make slurry-based fertilising cost-competitive with or cheaper than artificial fertiliser alone.

## 14. "...nother energy crisis as prices jump" (AgriBusiness energy feature)

Bord Gáis Energy, SSE Airtricity and Energia have all announced gas/electricity customer price hikes from October; Pinergy, Flogas, Yuno Energy and Electric Ireland have also increased charges in recent months. Gas prices have risen sharply since early 2026 (Figure 1 chart), with further wholesale price hikes already announced by energy suppliers suggesting more retail increases could follow. Ireland uses gas to produce over 40% of its electricity and is almost unique in Europe in having no effective gas stockpiles — major consumers (electricity generators, dairy co-ops) can hedge some costs via futures contracts, but there's no escaping a sustained gas-price increase as previous futures contracts roll off and replacement contracts match current (higher) prices. Reasons cited: the Middle East conflict and the ongoing Russian invasion of Ukraine have both constrained supply; Asian LNG demand (previously met by Gulf region exports) is increasingly served by US LNG exports, and the US has become more important for EU supplies as the EU cuts Russian imports — growing competition for those supplies has a knock-on effect on European prices. **EU gas stockpiles are at their lowest in more than 5 years** (68% full at time of writing vs an 84% five-year average for the same September week) after an exceptionally hot, dry summer curtailed nuclear generation (cooling issues) and renewables, replaced by gas-powered generation; EU rules require 90% storage capacity by 1 November, though countries can deviate during market turbulence. Goldman Sachs suggest gas prices might reach €100/MWh by December if the EU is forced through market purchases to meet demand rather than relying on summer-stockpiled gas. Separately: fuel/diesel prices also under pressure from Strait of Hormuz shipping tension, Red Sea attacks near Yemen, Saudi Arabia closing a crude pipeline (rerouting product via a Red Sea port, attacked Monday), and severe global refining capacity constraints for diesel.

## 15. "What will the COP achieve?" (Colm McCarthy, The Outside View opinion column)

Argues COP (Conference of the Parties) climate summits have become increasingly futile: the UN's Environment Programme recently declared an overshoot of the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C target (intended by 2030) as inevitable. Structural reason given: the UN has 193 sovereign members with limited incentive to contribute to others' climate costs, no powers of enforcement, and no real penalties for missing targets — climate costs/benefits aren't aligned at a national level (benefits accrue globally, costs are borne locally), unlike a single country managing its own affairs. The EU has pursued shared targets with some success but is not a superstate and has had its efforts unmatched outside Europe. China has the world's highest emissions by volume (1.4bn people, per-capita emissions ~2/3 of the US level), dwarfing the US total despite the US population being only 340m. Trump has withdrawn the US from the Paris Agreement, calls the IPCC's work "a hoax," and has criticised recent US gas price rises — US retail petrol price (~98c/litre in euro terms) is roughly half the EU/Ireland level, mostly due to VAT/excise rather than underlying cost differences, reflecting different public policy choices (more plentiful US supply via "drill, baby, drill" but no federal fuel-economy mandates). China, the EU and the US together account for ~51% of world emissions (33%/12%/6% respectively) and dominate world trade, giving them outsized leverage; expanding economies elsewhere (India, parts of Africa/Latin America) argue for exemption from the adjustment burden, wanting it to fall on already-developed countries — small countries with tiny emissions shares are tempted to focus on adaptation rather than continuing to push for mitigation given the pattern of failure. Next COP venues: Antalya, Türkiye (next month), then Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (2027).

## 16. "Case study one: when illness changes the succession plan" and "Case study two: helping a child buy their own farm" (Agricultural Relief/succession feature)

Two detailed worked tax-succession case studies, directly reinforcing the succession thesis already well-evidenced elsewhere in Cuan.

**Case study one:** a father farms into his late 70s, becomes ill, moves to a nursing home; his son takes over with no formal lease or written partnership agreement (common in families where everyone "just knows" who's farming the land, so paperwork is left aside). Eight years later the father dies, leaving the son a €2m farm. Because the father's own active farming had ceased years before death, Agricultural Relief and Business Relief can both be at risk — the current Section 89 Agricultural Relief test focuses on the recipient's position (80% farmer test, active farmer test) rather than requiring the person who owned the farm to have actively farmed it throughout the previous 6 years; the fact he'd stopped farming doesn't by itself prevent relief applying. If Agricultural Relief applies: the €2m farm's taxable value reduces to €200,000, covered by the son's €400,000 Group A threshold, no CAT payable. **Without Agricultural Relief**, the calculation is very different: €2m less the €400,000 threshold leaves €1.6m taxable at 33% — CAT of **€528,000**, typically funded not from the farm's own bank account but via substantial borrowing or selling land. A proposed **Section 89A** extension of the active-farmer test to the deceased parent (where the parent stopped farming due to ill health) would otherwise risk denying relief in genuinely common scenarios like this. Core message: families should not deliberately rely on informal arrangements, but the tax system also needs to recognise that real farming families don't always operate to a perfectly documented succession plan — worth reviewing the position now if someone has already stepped back and another family member has taken over, rather than waiting until a transfer/inheritance happens.

**Case study two:** a son returns home after years working in Australia, has saved €100,000, wants to establish his own farm; his parents aren't ready to transfer the home farm, so instead they gift him €500,000 conditional on it being invested in agricultural property within 2 years. He combines this with his own €100,000 to buy a €600,000 farm. Under current Agricultural Relief rules, a conditional gift/inheritance of money can qualify where conditions are met and investment happens within 2 years — Agricultural Relief reduces the €500,000's taxable value to €50,000, comfortably inside his €400,000 Group A threshold, no CAT payable. The proposed **Section 89A** would put this existing provision (conditional gift of money for agricultural investment) on a more secure footing. Without Agricultural Relief, only the first €400,000 would fall inside the Group A threshold, with the remaining €100,000 taxed at 33% (€33,000 CAT). Closing framing across both case studies: Agricultural Relief is generous because it has to be — a 90% valuation reduction sounds extraordinarily generous until you consider that a €2m farm valuation doesn't create €2m of actual cash; without the relief, an ordinary farm succession (illness, a child gradually taking over, parents helping a child establish a viable farm) can produce a tax liability bearing little relationship to the income the farm can generate, forcing a sale of land just to pay the tax. **Three questions every family should answer before a farm transfer:** (1) will the recipient pass the 80% test; (2) who will actually farm the land for the following 6 years; (3) is there any intention to sell/gift/lease differently or restructure part of the farm during that period — if any answer is unclear, work through the Agricultural Relief position before the deed is signed. Note: the most difficult succession cases are rarely ones where a family took advice too early — they're the cases where the farm was transferred, a lease changed, or farming ceased before anyone stopped to ask what that meant for the relief.

## 17. "Agricultural Relief: what it is and why does it matter to farm families?" (Marty Murphy, Head of Tax, ifac) — the explainer underlying case studies one and two above

Directly explains the mechanics behind item 16's worked examples. Core principle: the capital value of a farm and the income available from farming it are two very different things — a €2m farm doesn't mean €2m sitting in the farm bank account to pay a tax bill. Without Agricultural Relief, a child inheriting a €2m farm with no prior Group A gifts/inheritances and unable to claim relief faces CAT on €1.6m after the €400,000 Group A threshold, at 33% — **€528,000**. With relief, qualifying agricultural property's taxable value is reduced by 90% (a €2m farm becomes €200,000 for CAT purposes), which the full €400,000 threshold covers — **no CAT at all**. "Land values have risen considerably over the years, but the income generated by the farm has not necessarily risen with them."

**What is CAT:** Capital Acquisitions Tax, arising when someone receives a gift or inheritance; the recipient is generally liable; how much they can receive tax-free depends principally on relationship to the giver. Lifetime thresholds: Group A (most parent-to-child successions) €400,000; Group B €40,000; Group C €20,000. Gifts/inheritances between spouses/civil partners are exempt. Agricultural property includes farmland, pasture, woodland, farm buildings, appropriate farmhouses, livestock, bloodstock, machinery, crops, trees and farm-payment entitlements — but **shares in a farming company are not agricultural property for Agricultural Relief purposes**, though Business Relief may instead be available (relevant because company shares don't qualify for Agricultural Relief, so the two reliefs should be considered together where a farming business operates through a company).

**Two main tests farm families need to understand:**
1. **The 80% farmer test** — after receiving the gift/inheritance, at least 80% of the gross market value of everything the recipient owns must be agricultural property (the farm being transferred is included in that calculation). Being a farmer in the ordinary sense doesn't automatically mean someone passes this test — other personal assets (a house, savings, off-farm investments) count too, and most debts aren't simply deducted, though there's a specific allowance for borrowings used to buy/repair/improve a principal private residence (not agricultural property itself).
2. **The active farmer test** — for at least 6 years, the agricultural property must be farmed on a commercial basis with a view to profit. The recipient doesn't necessarily have to farm it themselves: alternatively, at least 75% of the market value must be leased to someone who meets the active-farmer requirements (a recognised agricultural qualification, or spending ≥50% of normal working time farming) — but the arrangement must be genuine, not just "somebody cutting the grass or grazing a few cattle" without properly farming the land, which does not automatically satisfy all conditions. Revenue's rules also allow for the qualification to be obtained *after* the gift/inheritance — a child doesn't necessarily have to give up an existing career and become a full-time farmer simply because they inherit the family farm.

**If the farm is sold within 6 years:** some or all of the Agricultural Relief claimed can be clawed back, unless the proceeds are reinvested in qualifying agricultural property within one year of the sale (a longer 6-year reinvestment period applies for a compulsory acquisition). An onward gift during the 6-year period also creates a clawback.

**Business Relief** (not limited to farms) can also reduce the taxable value of qualifying business property by 90% — particularly important where a farming business operates through a company, since company shares don't qualify for Agricultural Relief but may qualify for Business Relief. Where there's any doubt about which applies, both reliefs should be considered together, since they can sometimes produce the same result but don't work identically.

**Could these rules change?** Yes — **Section 89A was enacted in Finance Act 2024** as a replacement framework for the existing Agricultural Relief provisions, but has not yet commenced (awaiting a Ministerial order) — the existing Section 89 therefore remains in force while Section 89A awaits commencement. Its significance: it would introduce much greater emphasis on what happened *before* the farm was transferred, including conditions applying to the person transferring the property (directly relevant to case study one's scenario — a parent who stopped actively farming years before death due to illness). Farm families should continue watching for its commencement.

## 18. "Farmers cannot be sacrificed for an EU budget deal" — Declan O'Brien, Irish Farmers Journal News Correspondent

**Note: this article's text was sideways and partly illegible in the photograph — the below is a best-effort partial capture, not a verbatim summary.**

IFA President Francie Gorman told a regional CAP meeting in Claremorris, Co.
