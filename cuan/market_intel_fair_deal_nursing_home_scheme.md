# "Fair Deal can help you soldier on in your golden years" — Fair Deal explainer

Source: The Sunday Times Money, Kieran O'Daly, 23 Feb 2025. Captured by John 8 Oct 2026 — an older clipping, still a genuinely useful reference.

## What it says

**Core argument:** misinformation and "barstool advice" (often well-meaning, from friends/family/even some professionals who don't actually know the scheme) puts many people off applying for Fair Deal, causing unnecessary fear and financial harm. Tom Murray (Fair Deal Advice) says he's dealt with people wrongly told Fair Deal will take all their money, cost them their home/farm, leave nothing for their children, or that they can't apply because they have savings or don't own their home — **none of this is true.**

**What Fair Deal is:** introduced 2009, unique in Europe — guarantees nursing home care for anyone who applies, priced proportionate to means. All applicants pay something toward their care, capped against assets/income; the cap holds unless there's a significant change in means while in care (and a drop in income/assets during care entitles the person to apply for reassessment, possibly reducing their liability).

**The numbers:**
- 10,000+ applications/year; ~95% of nursing homes nationally are signed up; ~97% of residents use the scheme.
- Fair Deal pays homes a set weekly rate: **€1,400/week urban, €900/week rural.**
- Applicant pays up to **80% of income** + **7.5% of assets/year** toward care, for as long as needed.
- **The family home is capped at 7.5%/year for a maximum of three years** — meaning nobody ever pays more than **22.5% of their home's value**, regardless of how long they remain in care.
- Couples (married or cohabiting): all percentages halved — each liable for only 40% of income and 3.75% of assets.
- **Exemption on the first €36,000 of assets outside the family home** (€72,000 for a couple).

**Farmers/business owners — a specific, different rule:** liability on farm/business assets is capped at **three years**, but only if the asset is being passed to a suitable HSE-approved successor who guarantees to operate it for a minimum of six years. Otherwise liability on those assets is uncapped. Successors must show most of their income is earned from, and most of their time spent on, the farm/business; leased land/businesses don't qualify for successor relief.

**The asset-rich-but-income-poor bridge — a nursing home loan:** available to cover the gap where someone has the assets but not the income to meet their Fair Deal liability. Becomes repayable on death, or earlier if the property is sold/transferred or the applicant becomes bankrupt. Interest-free if repaid within six months of sale/transfer, or within 12 months of the applicant's death — becomes liable for interest after that.

**Gifting strategy, for the asset-rich/income-poor:** if more than five years from needing care, start gifting assets to reduce future liability. Total gifts received under €400,000 lifetime limit can be received by children gift-tax-free, and can help support the parent while using the gifted asset. **A gift is still assessed at 7.5% as part of the giver's own Fair Deal contribution**, but the children receiving it get 40% tax relief on it; without planning, families could otherwise lose significant sums over the course of care.

**How to apply:** two assessments — medical (establishes whether long-term nursing home care is actually required) and financial (establishes income/assets against liability). Fred Hickey (Fair Deal Advisors): anyone who anticipates needing care in the next few years should discuss it with loved ones and apply **immediately** if eligible — approval holds your place for six months, giving time to organise. Also recommends appointing an **Enduring Power of Attorney** in case of incapacity.

**The political/systemic concern Hickey raises:** nursing home rates, particularly in rural locations, need to rise significantly for Fair Deal to remain viable long-term — some rural operators are struggling against increasingly cost-dominated competition; cites Ceile Care's joint-purchasing scheme as a positive industry initiative helping contain costs for some rural operators.
