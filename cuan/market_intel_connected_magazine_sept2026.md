## Editor's Note — "If the AI bubble bursts, Ireland will feel it" (Charlie Taylor)
**Gartner forecast, cited via analysts John-David Lovelock and Ben Lee:**
- Worldwide AI spending to grow **49.5% this year alone**, reaching
  **$2.7 trillion (€2.38tn) in 2026**, up from $1.78tn last year;
  forecast to rise to **$3.63tn in 2027**. Almost half of this year's
  spend is AI infrastructure.
- "The buildout of AI data centre capacity is the largest infrastructure
  project humanity has ever undertaken" (Lovelock).
- A small number of the world's biggest tech companies (Amazon,
  Microsoft, Google-parent Alphabet named) spent a combined **$450
  billion** on infrastructure last year alone; expected to **double in
  2026** and reach **$1.4 trillion in 2027**. Many of these firms are
  funding this via **debt**.
- Global semiconductor revenue forecast to reach **$1.6 trillion this
  year** (+92% from $809bn in 2025), reaching $1.9tn by 2027; memory is
  the main driver (54% of total, up from 27% last year) — memory revenue
  alone forecast to reach $837bn this year and pass $1tn in 2027; DRAM
  revenue +246.6%, NAND flash +371.9%.
- AI data centres forecast to rise from **36.5% to 53%+ of chip revenue
  by 2030** — a major structural shift in where chip-industry value is
  created.
- **Ireland-specific risk flagged explicitly by the editor:** "Ireland
  has a stake in this given that many of these companies have big
  operations here... Tech multinationals pay a large share of our
  corporation tax. If there is an AI bubble, and if it bursts, we'll
  feel it more than most." Direct, on-the-record editorial warning
  about concentration risk in Ireland's corporation-tax base.
- Counter-view cited: Jamie Dimon (CEO, JPMorgan), speaking to CNBC
  recently: "In my own view, and I may be wrong, it'll ultimately play
  out and pay out... These people are doing real calculations. They see
  what it costs to do the frontier models. They see what it costs to do
  inferencing. The need is going up dramatically."

## "Founder Therapy" column — DC Cahalane (Sure Valley Ventures)
**DC Cahalane**, venture partner at SVV (Sure Valley Ventures, investing
exclusively in AI technologies), four-time founder, author of the
upcoming book *We're Doing This Wrong: How to Build Entrepreneur
Ecosystems That Actually Work*. Answers a submitted founder question:
"We've built brilliant tech but nobody's buying. Six pilots, none
converted. Half the team wants a VP of Sales, half says keep building.
Which is it?"

**Cahalane's core answer: neither — it's an information problem, not a
demand problem.** Key points, directly relevant to sales/commercial
development thinking:
- "A pilot that doesn't convert is a 'no' that nobody had the heart to
  say out loud" — pilots can look like progress for months while
  actually being unconverted rejections.
- **"Write down who signs"** — name, job title, budget line, and month
  the budget gets set for your five biggest opportunities; if you can't
  fill this in for three of five, "you haven't got a pipeline, you've
  got a list of people who were nice to you."
- **"Ring the ones that got away"** — call all lapsed pilots/prospects
  not to re-pitch, but to ask what actually happened and then stay
  silent until they answer; "founders hate this exercise, which is
  roughly why it works."
- **"Count the deals you closed yourself"** — if a founder hasn't closed
  deals personally, hiring a VP of Sales won't save the company, since
  you'd be handing over a sales process that doesn't yet exist.
  "Founder-led selling isn't the bit you suffer through until you can
  afford a grown-up. It's how the repeatable thing gets built in the
  first place."
- **On hiring a salesperson:** "a good salesperson multiplies whatever
  you already have. If there's nothing there, they multiply nothing" —
  don't hire until you can state in one sentence, without hedging, who
  buys the product and what breaks in their business if they don't.
- **On product differentiation:** being "20% better across the board
  loses to inertia every time" — the bar for switching from an
  incumbent needs to be "several times better at one thing that
  genuinely hurts," since switching costs are political, not just
  technical, and someone inside the buying organisation championed the
  incumbent.
- **The uncomfortable possibility boards avoid raising:** "you may have
  built something very good that nobody urgently needs this year" — a
  distinct problem from a sales-execution problem, and no roadmap or
  hire fixes it; only real buyer conversations reveal which problem you
  actually have.
- Final prescription: **"Five conversations this week, not five demos."**
  Ask what the prospect is doing about the problem today, what it's
  costing them, and who would need to sign off on fixing it.
