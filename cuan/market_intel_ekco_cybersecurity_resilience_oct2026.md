# Cybersecurity resilience over compliance — Declan Timmons, Ekco Ireland

**The shift described:** cybersecurity compliance is "no longer just
about proving that all the boxes are ticked" — organisations are now
expected to demonstrably **withstand, respond to and recover from**
cyberattacks, not just hold the right policies on paper. Quote from
Declan Timmons (managing director for professional services, Ekco
Ireland, founded 2016, ~1,000 employees globally): "the biggest
challenge organisations face today is the growing gap between the
speed of emerging threats and their ability to manage them."

**AI named as actively worsening the threat side:** "the speed and
complexity of these attacks are often fuelled by the widespread
adoption of AI by attackers to assist in the development and delivery
of their malware" — a direct, named example of AI as a threat
amplifier, not just a defensive tool.

**Regulatory driver — directly continues the already-logged NIS2
thread:** **DORA and NIS2** are explicitly named as the regulations
forcing this shift from a "periodic exercise focused on policies and
audits" to a genuine resilience mindset — boards and senior leadership
are increasingly accountable for cyber risk, "yet many organisations
are still developing the governance structures needed to manage those
responsibilities effectively."

**Practical recommendations given:**
- Treat compliance as an opportunity to build resilience into the
  organisation's DNA, not a regulatory burden.
- Have documented incident response plans in place — "a playbook ready
  to go."
- Run regular incident simulations so decision-makers are prepared
  before a real event.
- **Third-party/supplier risk flagged as one of the most important
  current cybersecurity challenges** — an organisation's security is
  "often only as strong as the security of its key suppliers." Boards
  should identify their most important suppliers, set clear security
  expectations, and monitor them on an ongoing basis, not as a
  one-time exercise.
- Cybersecurity is "increasingly recognised as a business risk rather
  than a purely technical issue" — boards expected to understand cyber
  risk, oversee resilience programmes, and make informed decisions
  about protecting critical services.
