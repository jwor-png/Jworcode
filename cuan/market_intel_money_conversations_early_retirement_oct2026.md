## 1. "Seven awkward conversations around money and how to have them" (Saoirse Hanley, p36)

- **Headline numbers (AIB-commissioned research, quoted):** 90% of people regularly let up to 50 euro slide rather than ask for it back; this "politeness premium" costs Irish consumers an estimated 600m euro a year; 4 in 10 adults say unspoken money issues have soured a relationship; adults aged 25 to 34 feel the most pressure (74% regularly overpay to avoid awkwardness). Thresholds before people speak up: Galway about 32.50 euro, Wexford about 12.50 euro.
- **Dan Bennett (global head of behavioural science, Ogilvy Consulting):** we overestimate how awkward asking will be and underestimate how likely people are to say yes; start small and practise; frame it as protecting the relationship; "the social loss of this feels bigger than the financial gain".
- **Kel Galavan (Mrs Smart Money) and Morgan O'Connell (Irish Independent money coach):** assume lag, not malice (90% of the time people are distracted, not stingy); remove friction (send the direct link or a Revolut request so it clears in two taps); be specific on repayment dates; agree restaurant choice before the booking if you are paying.
- **The seven situations covered:** (1) someone forgot to pay their share; (2) a friend paid last time then suggested a dearer restaurant; (3) you owe money but must wait until payday; (4) a friend joins rounds but never buys one; (5) someone paid you back but under-estimated the amount; (6) you drove on a road trip and nobody offered petrol money; (7) a group trip is booked and one person pulls out after the deposit. Each comes with suggested wording.
- **Product mention:** AIB's new "Zippay" lets users request money from a contact.

## 2. Money Coach: "My husband wants to retire early, but I don't think we can afford it" (Morgan O'Connell, p37)

- **The question:** husband wants to retire about four years early, using his tax-free lump sum and some joint savings to buy and renovate a doer-upper, claim the Vacant Property Refurbishment Grant, and rent it out to supplement retirement income. His pension pot is almost double hers.
- **O'Connell's advice (as printed):** separate the feelings from the practical questions (can both address fatigue without risking joint finances; can he afford to stop earlier); he does not have the right to use joint savings and she should expect her security not to be threatened; get professional advice from one financial planner both trust, with cashflow projections comparing the property plan against staying the course (working longer allows more contributions and leaves the pension invested longer).
- **Property risk points:** the grant is up to 70,000 euro but is not a straight swap for pension income; purchase, renovation and upfront costs, delays, cashflow risk, grant conditions and clawbacks, then finding tenants; landlord duties include tax, expenses and interruptions to rent. Money lost during working life can be replenished; in retirement it cannot.
