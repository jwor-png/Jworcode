# "How to write a condolence letter that connects" (The Sunday Times Magazine, pp54 to 55, 25 Oct 2026; Frish Brandt, adapted by Liz Edwards)

Source: The Sunday Times Magazine, 25 October 2026, Relationships, pp54 and 55. Captured by photograph from John, 28 Oct 2026. Extract from Brandt's book "Unfinished Business: How to Write the Five Essential Letters of Your Life" (Particular Books, 20 pounds, out Tuesday). Brandt is a 72-year-old "letter midwife" from San Francisco who works with private clients and in Stanford University classes. Her five essential letters: I love you, goodbye, I miss you, thank you and I'm sorry.

## What it says

- **AI:** Brandt knows how tempting it can be to reach for AI when writing challenging letters. While ChatGPT might work for admin, a condolence letter requires humanity and serves the writer as well as the recipient: "We are given our opportunity to sit with our recollections and say goodbye." More important than how, what or when is writing the letter at all.
- **Three things to think about before you write:** *Format:* a handwritten note is more appropriate than a text or email; if your handwriting is poor, print it, but always handwrite the recipient's name in the salutation, your name in the closing, and note the date. *Length:* depth, not length; even a few heartfelt words serve their purpose; when in doubt, imagine receiving it ("Grief weighs more when it is carried alone"). *Timing:* write soon and write often, but a letter is equally meaningful if it arrives after the initial flurry, when the world quiets down and the grieving are just beginning to experience "a world without".
- **What to write:** begin by acknowledging the loss and name the person who has died; express sympathy (attend to the other person, not your own loss); reflect on the qualities of the person who died (wit, honesty, generosity, wisdom, playfulness, resilience); share specific memories (grief can cloud memory, so your stories help the recipient build a portrait); mention the special qualities of the person you are writing to, reminding them of their strengths; offer specific assistance rather than "Let me know if I can help" (examples: bring a lasagne next week, check in early in the week, drive the kids to school, call on Tuesday mornings, offer company on a morning walk); sign off with a thoughtful word, phrase or quote.
- **Examples shared with Brandt:** "Words are insufficient, but they are the best we've got just now, so these will have to do." "Loss is a place of silence, the ineffable is everywhere. I am there, alongside you." "It is a fearful thing to love what death can touch" (quoted as said by a rabbi).
