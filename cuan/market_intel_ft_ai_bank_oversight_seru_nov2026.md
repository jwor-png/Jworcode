## What it says
- Post-mortems into Silicon Valley Bank's 2023 collapse prompted new thinking on US bank supervision. The Federal Reserve this month released plans for an overhaul; Michelle Bowman (Fed vice-chair for supervision) commissioned an independent review (the "Bowman report").
- Core finding: supervisors knew of the problems as early as 2022 (large interest-rate risk, many uninsured depositors) but did not take enough remedial action. Seru: the collapse "was not as much a problem of insufficient information as it was about supervisory will and courage to take tough decisions."
- Bowman's plan: clarify lines of responsibility, focus on big material risks, give supervisors tools to flag problems earlier, cut layers of supervisory committees for five regional supervisory leaders, and (with the OCC and FDIC) make it easier to share supervisory information more widely.
- Seru's argument: the pay-offs are asymmetric. A supervisor who acts and is wrong risks large personal and political costs; one who waits may see the risk disappear, or show up later when blame is harder to assign. That biases towards inaction, and the reforms do not address it.
- His proposals: use AI to change the default by making inaction visible. AI would not replace supervisory judgment but could analyse all the information gathered and flag whether risk has deteriorated materially. If supervisors then choose to wait they must explain why, making inaction "a decision for which someone would be accountable." A cultural change is needed: tolerate that some will get it wrong, and protect those who take reasonable decisions. As banks use AI to work out how to game regulation, supervisors should embrace AI too.
