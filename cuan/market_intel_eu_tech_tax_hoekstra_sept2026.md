# Market intel — EU Commissioner Hoekstra on Big Tech tax and energy windfall tax

**Source:** Sunday Independent, Business Interview, Sarah Collins,
27 September 2026, pp.6-7, ahead of Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra's visit
to Dublin. REPORTED — named-official interview; not independently
verified by Cuan.

## Who and context
**Wopke Hoekstra**, European Commissioner for Climate, Net Zero and
Clean Growth, also responsible for EU taxation policy. Former Dutch
finance minister (4+ years), Dutch foreign minister 2022, European
Commissioner since 2024; earlier career at Shell and McKinsey. Visiting
Dublin the week of 27 Sept 2026 for the Eurofi Financial Forum context
(same summit referenced in the Citi/McIntosh piece already logged) and
an informal EU energy ministers' meeting.

## Big Tech tax
Hoekstra says the EU will **"explore" a bloc-wide tax on Big Tech**
(Google, Meta, Apple named) if global OECD-led talks on digital taxation
drag on too long — "if there's one thing we all agree on... sovereignty
and tax are intrinsically linked. So this is a sovereign decision by the
European Union. Full stop." He has not closed the door on a digital
levy and questions the "viability" of OECD-led talks (OECD to publish a
progress report on digital taxation in December, 5+ years after
brokering the global 15% minimum tax deal, which remains hated by the
US).

**Ireland's position:** the Irish Government has said a tech tax would
be "deeply harmful" to Ireland and could stoke tensions with the US,
given Trump's threat to tariff any country/region introducing a
"digital services tax" he sees as unfairly targeting US firms. Tánaiste/
Finance Minister **Simon Harris** says Ireland's tax take is "a matter
for Ireland" and that Ireland's economic success "benefits Europe."
French Europe minister Benjamin Haddad and finance minister Roland
Lescure have pushed back, saying taxing rights for large firms should be
"more fairly distributed" among EU partners and any tech levy should
"weigh more" on foreign firms.

**Figures cited:** company filings show Apple paid ~€15bn in taxes in
Ireland last year, Microsoft close to €6bn. The 2024 global 15% minimum
tax deal has brought Ireland an extra €1.1bn from US subsidiaries paying
the higher rate (its first full year in place); Department of Finance
earlier estimated a digital levy could cost Ireland up to €2bn/year.
Public opinion: 75%+ of EU/Irish citizens believe large multinationals
should pay a minimum tax amount (2025 Eurobarometer survey); a separate
2026 Fair Tax Foundation survey found Europeans split on an EU-wide tech
tax and on whether proceeds should fund the bloc's budget.

**EU budget context:** European Commission weighing a lump-sum levy on
companies with €100m+ annual turnover (could raise ~€7bn/year, opposed
by Ireland, Germany, Luxembourg and others, seen as unlikely to proceed);
alternative MEP-backed tax on tech firms' online advertising revenue
(could raise ~€5bn/year, France-backed, not formally on the table). This
sits within the EU's next 7-year budget (2028-2034 MFF) negotiations,
which Ireland is chairing discussions on next month under its EU
presidency — cross-referencing the CAP/MFF impasse already logged
(`market_intel_farm_land_tax_succession_cap_batch.md`).

## Energy windfall tax
Hoekstra travelling to Dublin for an informal EU energy ministers'
meeting, where he'll be asked to consider a new windfall tax on fuel
companies given prices/profits rising amid the ongoing US-Iran conflict.
Finance ministers from six EU countries (Germany, Austria, Italy,
Portugal, Spain, Poland) wrote to Simon Harris last month asking him to
intervene; German finance minister Lars Klingbeil has also pressed the
issue at a European Commission meeting chaired by Harris in Dublin
Castle last week — to be revisited at an October 9 Luxembourg finance
ministers' meeting, also Tánaiste-chaired.

Hoekstra says he's "open to the idea" given "outrageous" energy-producer
profits, but flags it's legally tricky and lacks unanimous EU country
backing; notes a 2022 EU "solidarity tax" on companies (post-Russia's
invasion of Ukraine) raised ~€267m in Ireland, but the European
Commission is less keen to repeat that route now. Poland is moving ahead
unilaterally; the Commission faces a live "dilemma" given ongoing legal
battles with energy firms over the 2022 solidarity levy — Dutch and
German subsidiaries of ExxonMobil have launched proceedings against the
EU, among unnamed others. Ireland's own energy regulator (CRU) said
earlier this year there's no evidence of excess profits being made by
companies here; CSO data shows wholesale electricity costs rose 77% in
the year to August, largely driven by gas prices and infrastructure
costs (cross-reference: this reinforces the Cost of Business Advisory
Forum's energy-cost findings already logged).

Hoekstra separately warned, ahead of UN Climate Week New York, that
countries "no longer have a chance" to meet the Paris 1.5°C target and
that recent summers are "probably the coolest of the years to come."
