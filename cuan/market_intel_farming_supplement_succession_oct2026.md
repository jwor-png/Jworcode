# Farming supplement, 6 Oct 2026 — Budget 2027 agri detail + succession/tenancy legal advice

## 1. Budget 2027: TB compensation boost and tax incentives (Margaret Donnelly)

Confirms and adds detail to the farm succession measures already logged in full (`market_intel_farm_succession_budget2027_oct2026.md`, `market_intel_budget_2027_full_oct2026.md`):

- **TB compensation ceiling up €500, to €3,500 per reactor animal** — follows sustained pressure from farm organisations, who argued the previous limit hadn't kept pace with sharply rising cattle values (particularly breeding stock) over the past two years, leaving herd owners significantly out of pocket.
- **Succession Farm Partnership annual tax credit confirmed doubling, €5,000 → €10,000** — consistent with the main paper's figure already logged.
- **Government "preparing to reduce" the waiting period before a land transfer can take place under a partnership** — this supplement frames it as still in train at time of writing, whereas the main Budget 2027 supplement (logged separately) reported the three-year holding period as already removed. Worth treating the "removed" framing as the more authoritative/final one given it's from the main budget-day coverage, but noting the discrepancy.
- **Scale context, a different and lower figure than previously logged:** only ~3,500 **registered farm partnerships** nationally (this is the broader category, distinct from the ~162 *active Succession* Farm Partnerships specifically already logged) — described as "a steady erosion," prompting renewed calls for Government intervention to halt the decline before it worsens further.
- **Tillage sector flagged as under severe, specific pressure**: additional funding promised for the Targeted Agricultural Modernisation Scheme (TAMS), though tillage-sector sources indicated ahead of the budget that the measures targeted at the struggling sector fell short of what's needed. Record demand from farmers investing in solar panels, low-emission slurry spreading equipment, dairy infrastructure improvements and environmental upgrades has come under severe pressure in recent years amid rising costs.
- **The Commission on Generational Renewal in Farming** (a body not previously named in Cuan's files) has highlighted succession as "one of the biggest long-term threats facing Irish agriculture," alongside escalating production costs, volatile grain prices, and growing competition for rented land.
- **Separate item, same page — Bluetongue virus (BTV):** spreads on the wind up to 150km; sheep and other ruminant farmers urged to consult their vet about vaccination ahead of a booster next year; 2,309 BTV cases detected across the UK, with vaccination push aiming to get ahead of further spread to Ireland. No venture tie, logged for completeness given the agribusiness sector lens.

## 2. "I've rented this land for years. Do I have any rights now that the owner has died?" (Mary Frances Fahy, Legal Advice)

A reader (20 years' informal/verbal lease on land adjacent to their own farm, landowner recently died, no written lease, worried about continuity of tenancy and rent payment with no local family to pay) gets detailed legal guidance from Mary Frances Fahy (solicitor/tax consultant, Fahy Neilan Solicitors, Roscommon) — **the same columnist who authored the farm succession sibling dispute piece already logged** (`market_intel_farm_succession_sibling_dispute_sept2026.md`).

Key points of general legal reference value:
- **No squatters' rights available** here — the reader went into possession with the landowner's express consent and has been paying rent for 20 years, which itself acknowledges the landowner's title; this rules out any adverse-possession claim.
- **On the landowner's death, an informal/verbal arrangement (no written lease) converts to a periodic (annual) tenancy** once any original lease term expired — entitling the tenant to at least **six months' notice** before termination.
- **The landowner's estate must be formally administered** (Grant of Probate or Grant of Administration Intestate) before new owners can legally serve a Notice to Quit — this process typically takes at least a year, often longer, especially without a will.
- **Practical guidance for finding who to pay/deal with:** check with local solicitors handling the estate; look for clues via the death notice; consider attending an anniversary Mass if the deceased was from the locality — a relative is almost always "waiting in the wings."
- **The Landlord and Tenant (Amendment) Act 1980** gives tenants rights to a lease renewal after five years of continuous occupation, subject to the property qualifying as a "tenement" (at least part must be covered by buildings) — in this reader's case, land only, no buildings, so this specific statutory renewal right likely doesn't apply, though occupation itself doesn't prevent being entitled to a new lease by other means.
- **Improvements made to rented land are very difficult to claim compensation for** — the advice is blunt: "you will have to pay the market rent and will not be entitled to claim for improvements made to the land" unless this was expressly agreed in a written lease at the outset (which didn't happen here).
- Practical closing suggestion: given the tenant has maintained/improved the land well for 20 years and new owners will know good tenants are hard to find, **purchasing the land outright** may be a genuine option worth exploring, especially if there are multiple non-local beneficiaries who may prefer to sell rather than continue renting.

## 3. "I'm thinking of going into a farm partnership — how do I avoid being left with nothing?" (Seán McGovern, Farm Finance)

A young farmer considering a partnership with a relative (relative provides land, farmer contributes time/work/money) seeks advice before committing. Seán McGovern (Cavan-based IFAC partner) gives detailed, directly reusable structuring guidance — **genuinely strong, practical succession-advisory material, worth treating as a primary reference alongside the Agricultural Relief explainer already logged.**

**Core structures explained:**
- **Registered Farm Partnership** — most common structure; landowner retains land ownership and licenses its use to the partnership; a Farm Partnership Agreement records each partner's contributed assets and the profit-share ratio; a separate **On Farm Agreement** covers day-to-day duties, hours, decision-making and how profit-share/drawings change over time. Must be registered with DAFM (Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine), with annual paperwork submitted by a specific deadline; minimum partnership term is **five years** to meet Registered Farm Partnership criteria.
- **A lease vs. a partnership answer different questions**: a lease gives rights to *use* the land (landowner retains sole control, relation receives a set per-acre amount, investor has no stake in how the farm is run, and any capital investment in buildings/improvements reverts to the landowner at lease end unless otherwise agreed and compensated); a partnership sets out how the farming *business* is owned and run jointly.
- **Succession Farm Partnership** — a further, distinct stepping-stone arrangement: a legally binding plan to transfer **80% of the land to a qualifying successor within a set timeframe**, carrying a **€25,000 tax credit available over five years**. Strict eligibility criteria apply; requires professional advice from both an accountant and a solicitor. Not relevant if there's no actual intention for the contributing partner to eventually acquire the land.

**Protecting the investing partner's position — the practical mechanics:**
- Capital invested can be recorded as a **capital account or a loan** owed back to the investing partner, with repayment terms set out in the agreement.
- Livestock/machinery contributed need **agreed opening values** with the accountant, and it must be clearly distinguished whether these assets belong to one partner only or to the partnership itself.
- **A practical approach to protecting improvements**: agree in advance who pays for each proposed project, who owns any removable equipment, and the exact compensation basis if the partnership ends — repaying the agreed unrecovered cost over an agreed useful life, with an independent valuation mechanism for disputes. McGovern's own blunt line: **"I would not fund a major shed on a promise to 'sort it out later.'"**
- **Death/incapacity provisions are essential, not optional**: the agreement needs a winding-up procedure covering valuation date, treatment of stock/crops/machinery/cash/debts/tax/grants, who may continue farming, how an independent valuer is appointed, and payment timing/security if spread over time. Without this, default partnership law can force dissolution on death, which serves neither the surviving partner (needs a workable right to continue) nor the deceased partner's estate (needs a defined payment).
- **Recommended starting discipline, before engaging solicitors**: prepare a **five-year cashflow modelled across three scenarios** — normal trading, a poor farming year, and an exit after year three — and a simple one-page outline covering expected investment, rewards and payback, before commissioning the full legal agreements. Also check less obvious risks: who can borrow/give guarantees or security, potential DAFM grant clawbacks, and any existing loans/charges over the land.
- **Registered Farm Partnerships can qualify for enhanced stock relief**, with a young trained farmer potentially qualifying for a higher relief rate if eligibility criteria are met — DAFM young-farmer support also carries its own qualification/control conditions, not to be assumed automatically secured.
