# Shareholder rights and AI safety — CalPERS general counsel, FT opinion

**Author's standing:** Prashant Yerramalli is general counsel of the
California Public Employees' Retirement System (CalPERS), which
invests more than **$650bn** and votes on thousands of shareholder
proposals annually. Former SEC Commissioner staffer and chief of staff
to SEC chair Gary Gensler.

**The core argument:** as AI companies race toward trillion-dollar
public stock offerings, their own executives warn the technology is
developing faster than society's capacity to oversee it — yet the
SEC has proposed **repealing Rule 14a-8**, the rule requiring public
companies to put qualifying shareholder proposals up for a vote,
arguing it's a matter for state law instead. The author argues this
could not come at a worse time for AI companies specifically.

**Historical/legal context:** Rule 14a-8 reflects a long-standing
bargain — raising public money in America has required giving
investors a forum to debate urgent issues, even when management
disagrees. The rule was already loosened once during the first Trump
administration (raised ownership thresholds, allowed barring repeat
proposals that failed to gain sufficient support) — the SEC at the
time still acknowledged a need to balance efficient company decision-
making against shareholder voice. Full repeal would go further,
removing the voice mechanism altogether.

**Why this specifically matters for AI companies:** the directors and
executives running America's AI companies are making decisions with
unprecedented stakes — how fast to deploy new models, to whom the
technology should be available, how to weigh development-delay costs
against risk, all while competing against China's own pace. The
author notes several AI labs began as non-profit or public-benefit
entities, and some leaders have themselves called for federal
oversight of their own work — evidence industry leaders already
recognise they need outside input to get these calls right.

**Core line of the piece:** "If AI companies make decisions without
robust input from shareholders, both sides will regret it." Rarely-
binding shareholder proposals still matter because they force board
attention onto issues investors care about and surface discussions
"from which both sides learn a great deal" — boards can still decide
differently, but should do so knowingly, not by preventing the
question being raised at all.

**Practical point on investor options:** selling shares isn't a
realistic response to disagreement for an investor the size of
CalPERS (which holds positions across the whole economy) or for small
investors either, given how broadly AI will touch nearly every
American life — strengthening the case for voice over exit.
