# "House prices have peaked, but don't hold out for a large drop" — Alan Ahearne

Source: Sunday Independent, Niamh Horan, 4 Oct 2026. Dr Alan Ahearne — professor of economics, University of Galway, former economic adviser to Taoiseach Micheál Martin, and the economist credited with predicting the 2008 property crash — speaking directly to the paper, named and on record, not a secondary summary.

## What it says

- **Core call:** house prices in Ireland have peaked, but buyers hoping for a significant drop (10-20-30%) are very unlikely to get one. "It's hard to imagine any sort of significant fall in house prices" — would require a huge deterioration in economic conditions and big increases in unemployment.
- **Expected path: a flatline, or a slow increase more in line with inflation (~2%)**, not the double-digit increases of recent years (which have "gone, which is good").
- Reacting to last month's ESRI report stating Irish property is **overvalued by 17%** — Ahearne pushes back on how loaded the word "overvalued" is; he says it's true prices are too high relative to income, particularly for middle earners, but that doesn't mean a rapid market "correction" is coming.
- **His actual affordability mechanism:** not falling prices, but **wages outstripping house prices over time** — "a reasonable expectation is that the Irish economy will continue to do well... and wage inflation will eventually rise faster than house prices." Wages ~3-4% growth, house prices flat or slower, affordability restored gradually that way instead.
- **Global risk flagged, not a prediction:** America's $40tn debt and rising government borrowing costs (US borrowing costs at a 20-year high) as a macro risk worth watching — "it's definitely a risk... worth keeping an eye on," though explicitly not something he's predicting will happen.
- **Current market data cited alongside the piece:** national residential sale prices rose 3% in the year to September 2026 (down from 5.8% the same period last year) — average national asking price €445,000. Sherry FitzGerald data: second-hand home values rose 1.1% July-September, 4.4% annually.

Worth holding both views side by side rather than treating either as settled — they're not strictly contradictory (a national flatline is consistent with Dublin specifically falling while other regions rise, which is exactly McWilliams's own regional-divergence point), but the *tone* differs: McWilliams frames this as a genuine "turn," Ahearne frames it as a healthy cooling with no crash risk.
