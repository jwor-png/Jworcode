# "A speak-up culture is only built by what happens after someone speaks" — Gina London, The Communicator

Source: Sunday Independent Business, "The Communicator" column, Gina London — same columnist already logged twice previously (inbox-anxiety piece, 30 Aug; presentation-delivery/Poland market-entry piece, 27 Sept). VERIFIED named columnist, citing a specific current news story (Cornell University allegations) and named academic research.

## What it says

**Opening framing — a question to leaders:** "Tell me more. What are you not seeing that you believe is wrong?" Most leaders will insist they want to hear ideas, questions, concerns or mistakes raised. Her point: psychological safety doesn't mean every idea is agreed with — it means someone feels able to admit a mistake, challenge an assumption, challenge an inappropriate joke, point out uncomfortable data, or simply say "I don't agree." Leaders build that trust (or fail to) well before the stakes become enormous — by how they react in the hundreds of small, low-stakes moments that precede any serious one.

**The news story used as the hook:** her 18-year-old daughter was talking to her about allegations surrounding Cornell University this week. The allegations are deeply disturbing — a former Cornell student, identified as Jane Doe, has filed a civil lawsuit alleging that she was drugged and gang raped by members of a fraternity and that the fraternity renewed attention after details of a case received renewed attention after a 2024 Snapchat group chat (named "Rodeo") surfaced, in which members of the fraternity allegedly discussed and bragged about assaults. Cornell says it previously investigated and imposed suspensions/expulsions, but the case remains contested and the local district attorney has now reopened criminal investigation.

**Her point, not about the allegations themselves, but the surrounding silence:** "That alone had travelled far more than what is happening in her own world dominating American news." Her strategy critique: "the strategy is flawed. A deadline is impossible. A colleague is repeatedly interrupted. A customer is being badly served. A junior employee sees a risk the senior team has missed. Every one around the table knows the meeting ends with nodding heads. Then, in the corridor afterwards, everybody suddenly has plenty to say." When that happens repeatedly, silence becomes a habit — "people come and which conversations are welcome and which opinions are welcome, are trained without realising they have created the corridor."

**Central argument (the headline point):** leaders can find themselves surrounded by agreement without ever understanding why. **Nobody needs to be explicitly told not to challenge the CEO.** "Does everyone agree?" is the easiest question to fail — "what are we missing?" and "who sees this differently" are the terrible questions no one wants to be asked. Leaders need to stop asking questions that make agreement the easiest answer. Try "what are we not saying that we needs to be said?"

**"Invite the disagreement":** culture is curated through hundreds of small moments that show people whether speaking up gets rewarded or ignored. Every group develops norms about what gets tolerated and what gets challenged and decides who gets rewarded and what gets ignored. Every time silence is rewarded, that reinforces caution about what happens next time. Leaders cannot suddenly demand courage when the stakes become enormous — leaders have to make room for people to practise speaking up before it happens, in an ordinary work environment, decades researching psychology.

**"Your reaction is the culture too" (named academic research cited):** Harvard Business School professor Amy Edmondson has spent decades researching psychological safety. She describes it as an environment where people believe speaking up is an act of heroism. It shouldn't require an act of heroism, it should be part of the job.

**"Silence communicates too":** London is explicit she's not equating the appalling allegations at Cornell with ordinary workplace dynamics — but the story illustrates the power of a group, and what it takes for one person to speak up against it. Reading the reported detail of the group chat (left to the reporters and the courts to establish) made her think about the power of a group in creating, or suppressing, a "speak-up culture." We talk a great deal about building a "speak-up culture" — companies put words like "candour, courage and transparency" on walls and websites, CEOs assure employees "my door is always open" — but a speak-up culture isn't created by words or an open-door policy, it's created by what actually happens after someone speaks.

**"Practise when the stakes are smaller":** perhaps most importantly, we need to practise speaking up before stakes become enormous. Speaking up shouldn't require an act of heroism. It should be part of the job.
