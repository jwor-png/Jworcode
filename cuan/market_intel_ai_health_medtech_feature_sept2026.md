## 1. Veonet Ireland — "Tech with a clinical purpose"
**Frank Doheny, CEO, Veonet Ireland.** Founded 2024. New purpose-built
ophthalmology clinic opened Carrickmines, April 2026, covering cataract
surgery, glaucoma, medical retina, vitreoretinal surgery, corneal care,
plus advanced diagnostics (OCT, retinal imaging, visual field testing,
pachymetry, Pentacam imaging) across glaucoma, AMD and diabetic
retinopathy. Doheny's framing: AI's near-term role is supporting
image analysis/diagnosis/monitoring, not replacing clinical judgement;
day-to-day clinical AI use "is still developing." Claimed capacity:
clinicians can perform surgeries and clinically treat up to **120
patients per day**. Plans a mobile ophthalmology unit later in 2026 to
extend diagnostic/treatment capability into communities, explicitly
framed against Sláintecare's community-care objective and reducing
waiting times.

## 2. TEKenable — "AI revolution moves into action"
**Peter Rose, co-founder and CIO, TEKenable.** Founded 2002, 200 staff.
Core argument: AI's most immediate healthcare impact isn't glamorous
diagnostics but freeing clinicians from admin/documentation/reporting
so they can spend more time on skilled work — "the near-term
opportunity in healthcare really is saving time for skilled
professionals." Sees longer-term potential in AI shifting healthcare
from treatment to prevention, using continuous data streams (glucose
monitors, wearables, electronic records) to flag gradual patient
deterioration and improve population-level resource planning.
**Named barriers flagged:** cybersecurity ("probably the number-one
issue... it becomes a patient safety issue, not just an IT issue" as
healthcare becomes more connected via EHRs/remote monitoring/connected
devices); cost/pricing unpredictability of LLM-based AI tools making
procurement difficult for a national health service; and a **scaling
gap** — cites an Irish radiologist and a PhD student who built AI
software converting CT scans into pseudo-MRI images for spinal injury
assessment (useful where CT scanners are common but MRI scarce), which
remains stuck at the two-person proof-of-concept stage with "no clear
pathway to scale that innovation across the health service." Rose's
prescription: a properly funded, resourced pathway to assess, trial,
measure and scale innovation generally, not just for AI.

## 3. Forvis Mazars — "Putting AI to work in healthcare"
**Elaine Daly, partner and head of business consulting, Forvis Mazars**
(950+ staff Ireland, founded 1945). Describes AI already delivering
value in clinical decision support, medical imaging/diagnostics
(X-ray, CT, MRI, pathology slides), and administrative burden reduction
(consultation notes, discharge summaries, referral letters). Flags the
core integration challenge: healthcare data/systems (EHRs, labs,
imaging, pharmacy, admin) typically operate in isolation, so "without
integration, AI remains confined to isolated pilots rather than
delivering value at scale" — connecting historical, clinical and
demographic data across sources is where "the magic really starts to
happen." **Marc Balbirnie (data/analytics lead, Forvis Mazars)** adds
that cloud platforms and interoperability standards (FHIR, HL7, MLOps/
DevOps) are increasingly important, alongside security/data sovereignty
(where sensitive patient data is stored/processed, especially with
AI models/infrastructure outside the EU) and specific EU AI Act
requirements for high-risk healthcare applications. Daly: "the most
effective approach is to build governance into AI initiatives from the
outset rather than attempting to retrofit controls later," citing GDPR,
the EU AI Act, sector-specific regulations, model validation, privacy/
security controls and transparent documentation as required layers.
Both sources frame the next phase of healthcare AI as "less about
experimentation and more about integration: putting reliable, governed
technology into the hands of professionals."
