# "Is the Irish housing market beginning to turn?" — David McWilliams

Source: Irish Times, Weekend "Last Word" column, David McWilliams (economist, named national commentator). VERIFIED — citing named primary data (Daft Q3 2026 report).

## What it says

**Early signs of a turn:**
- National asking prices up 3.0% year-on-year — about half last year's 5.8% pace, meaning sellers are adjusting down their expectations.
- Agreed sale prices up just 0.8% — the slowest in six years.
- The premium buyers were willing to pay over asking price has narrowed from 6.8% to 2.6% — buyers are no longer being bullied into bidding wars out of fear of being outbid.
- Per Daft Q3 2026: Dublin sale prices fell 5.2% year-on-year, the largest annual fall since 2014, accelerating over summer (-2% in the three months to September); asking prices still up 2.7%, so the asking/sale-price gap is widest in Dublin of any region.

**Why — not driven by supply:**
- McWilliams: "before you go thinking the housing crisis has been solved, hold on. What we are seeing is not evidence of that." Prices remain 44% above pre-Covid levels. Two actual drivers: (1) buyers have hit the limit of what income/lending rules/support schemes allow them to pay; (2) what's being built doesn't match what people want — still mostly 3-bed semi-detached homes in commuter towns, when Irish households increasingly want urban apartments (smaller households, more singles/couples not wanting extra bedrooms).
- "The cure for high prices is high prices" — eventually very high prices self-correct by depressing demand once buyers run out of money, which should prompt higher supply and falling prices — but in Ireland right now, buyers are simply "maxed out," so they can't keep bidding up prices even if they wanted to.

**Regional price moves since Covid:**
- Connacht/Ulster (traditionally most moribund market): +72% since Covid.
- Munster outside the cities: +72%.
- Leinster outside Dublin: +62%.
- Four regional cities combined: +57%.
- Dublin: +32% (lags behind).
- Current asking price for a 3-bed semi: Dublin €635,000; Cork city €431,000; Galway city €424,000; Limerick city €376,000; Waterford city €313,000.

**Affordability — the actual numbers:**
- First-time buyers (half of all buyers) can borrow at most 4x income with a 10% deposit.
- Example couple in their 30s, both on average earnings (€1,046.88/week each, €54,400/year, €108,900 combined): max mortgage 4x income = €435,500; adding 10% deposit, max purchase price ≈ **€484,000**.
- A Dublin 3-bed semi (€635,000) is out of reach on income alone — would need either a dual income around €145,000, or roughly €200,000 in cash.
- A Dublin 2-bed apartment (avg €406,000) is within lending limits and repayments would be under 20% of gross income — the real challenge there is saving the ~€41,000 cash deposit (≈4.5 months combined gross pay), not affordable but not insurmountable, especially with family help (common for parents to gift a deposit).
- A 3-bed apartment in an urban setting (€502,000) is about €18,000 over the couple's maximum threshold, requiring ≈€66,500 in cash — "even if taking the completely stretched first-time buying couple... they are still well short of the needed funds in Dublin."

**McWilliams's policy conclusion:** "This is a numbers game, and the numbers don't add up. We need far more apartments in our cities, and the asking prices for these apartments must fall gains [sic — likely 'further']." Calls for the State to fast-track building in cities, build up (more floors = cheaper apartments per unit), and reduce the ability of "serial objectors to put spurious spanners in the works." Closing view: "the market has spoken — prices are adjusting downwards, because the cure for high prices is high prices. Long may this last."
