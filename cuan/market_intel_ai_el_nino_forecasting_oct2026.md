# AI and El Niño forecasting — FT Big Read, 17/18 Oct 2026

**The hook:** this year's El Niño is "the strongest on record in
absolute terms" (UN World Food Programme earth-observation expert
Giancarlo Pini calls it a "Godzilla" El Niño). WMO expects it to peak
December, persisting to at least February 2027; sea surface
temperature in the Niño 3.4 tracking region forecast at 3.7°C above
the long-term average in December — highest since 1950s records
began.

**The AI pitch:** faster, cheaper forecasting of extreme weather than
traditional supercomputer-based modelling, now being used across
agriculture, humanitarian response and commodity trading.

**Named real-world applications:**
- **World Food Programme** — Pini's team models where food shortages
  might occur by combining traditional forecasts with AI, distributed
  to WFP offices/governments so farmers and stakeholders get early
  warning; August prediction: El Niño could contribute to 274m more
  people being acutely food-insecure by end of 2027 across 45
  countries (separately, Climate Impact Lab — joint UC/Chicago/
  Rutgers research — predicted at least 451,000 heat-related deaths
  linked to this El Niño, Nigeria/Indonesia/Sudan among worst
  affected).
- **International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies**
  — using AI to better model demand for funding over coming months;
  cites early warning this time as better than ever before.
- **TomorrowNow** — a US non-profit combining forecasts from Google
  and Tomorrow.io with government meteorological/research institution
  data, delivering agricultural advice directly to farmers via text
  message — has reduced the cost of running forecasting models,
  making it possible to distribute advice for **under $1 per farmer
  per year** (Brian Miranda, CEO).
- **Moreton Capital Partners** — an investment firm using AI to spot
  commodity-market opportunities from El Niño disruption, launched a
  dedicated fund in June. Brazilian hedge funds reportedly used AI
  "more precisely than the sugar industry" (Murilo Mello, Hedgepoint)
  to reposition sugar futures ahead of supply disruption.
- **Panama** — declared a state of emergency in August; El Niño cut
  vessel traffic through the Panama Canal; the last El Niño
  (2023-2024) cost Panama $1bn in lost revenue from reduced maritime/
  agricultural traffic.

**The scientific pushback, genuinely balanced in the piece:**
- WMO deputy secretary-general Ko Barrett: "the AI approach is at
  least equal to our traditional modelling approaches, but that's
  trained on the past — and the future may not be the past."
  Liz Stephens (Reading University, climate risk): risk is
  "unprecedented weather extremes in locations which might be
  preparing only for the extremes they have experienced in the past."
- **UK Met Office research (Adam Scaife)**: AI may be *less* suited
  than physics-based models for El Niño specifically — when fed 2009
  El Niño data, a physics-based model correctly predicted the very
  cold northern-Europe winter that followed; the AI model did not.
  "We don't fully understand why the AI models don't do a good job of
  winter and why they fail... they are not as good as the physics-
  based models at that extreme."
- Scott Hosking (Alan Turing Institute): AI is "part of the solution
  but exactly how is an open question," given the sheer range of
  El Niño effects worldwide needing forecasting across "tens of
  thousands of different future scenarios."
- Forecasting is explicitly called "an inexact science" — El Niño's
  severity doesn't necessarily correlate with damage (worst effects
  can hit unpopulated areas or occur at less significant times of
  year), and other phenomena like the Indian Ocean dipole complicate
  matters further.
