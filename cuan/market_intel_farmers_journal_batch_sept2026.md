## 1. "Financial planning for the long term is essential" (Lorcan
Roche Kelly, Focus: Pensions and Taxes intro)
Framing piece for a pensions/tax special section. Core argument:
farming income is increasingly volatile (geopolitical/climate-driven),
which complicates the normally straightforward task of pension/tax
planning. Key points: strong-income years should be used to fund
retirement, not spent immediately ("if I have, I'll spend it" attitude
reduces retirement comfort); minimising the annual tax bill isn't
always the most financially efficient move (e.g. buying a tractor
purely as a tax write-off isn't automatically the best use of a good
year's income); explicitly states these are educational articles, not
personalised financial advice.

## 2. "Pensions: the unbeatable savings plan" (Lorcan Roche Kelly)
- Pension contributions remain the most tax-efficient savings vehicle
  available; income after retirement is taxable, but 25% of a pension's
  value can be withdrawn tax-free at retirement, **capped at €200,000**.
- Age-banded tax-free contribution limits (% of income): **under 30:
  15%; 30-39: 20%; 40-49: 25%; 50-54: 30%; 55-59: 35%; 60+: 40%**
  (Figure 1 in the clipping).
- Income threshold for tax-free contribution purposes: **€115,000**
  (so someone over 60 can contribute up to €46,000/year tax-free;
  someone under 30, up to €17,250). Lifetime threshold: **€2.2m, rising
  to €2.8m by 2029**.
- Tax relief only applies to income tax — **does not cover PRSI or
  USC**.
- For incorporated farm businesses: a **Personal Retirement Savings
  Account (PRSA)** can allow contributions of up to 100% of a
  director's salary in a single year — a genuine planning opportunity,
  though rules have changed several times recently; independent
  financial-adviser input recommended before acting.
- **Auto-enrolment (started this year, 2026):** all employees aged
  23-60 earning over €20,000/year across all income sources must be
  enrolled unless already in a suitable company scheme — this
  explicitly **includes farmers who have incorporated their business
  and are an employee of their own farm.** Contributions are 3:3:1
  (employee:employer:State, i.e. the State adds €1 for every €7 in the
  pot). Ratio starts at 1.5%/1.5%/0.5% (years 1-3) and rises in stages
  to 6%/6%/2% by year 10+ (2036) — full schedule in Table 1 of the
  clipping.
- **State Pension:** available from age 66, can now be delayed to age
  70 for a higher weekly payment. Contributory pension requires 2,080
  full-rate PRSI contributions (40 years) for the maximum rate, minimum
  520 contributions for any non-contributory pension. Current rates:
  contributory €299.30/week (under 80) / €309.30 (80+); non-
  contributory (means-tested) €288/week (66-80) / €298/week (80+).

## 3. "Tax planning is about efficiency, not saving" (Lorcan Roche
Kelly)
- Ireland's October Budget expected to include tax-band/credit changes
  — the article notes farmers/businesses need to plan for potential
  changes to PAYE, USC, and personal tax-deductible allowances (nursing
  home expense relief specifically flagged as rising to 40% for
  qualifying cases).
- **A different framing of "efficiency":** minimising tax owed and
  maximising legitimate spending/investment in the business are
  different goals, and the article stresses efficient tax planning
  should be led by sound business/investment decisions, not the other
  way around — a farmer shouldn't make a purchase purely to reduce a
  tax bill if it isn't otherwise a good investment.
- **Farm-specific reliefs flagged:** VAT refunds/reliefs on
  qualifying farm partnership/civil partnership arrangements; capital
  gains tax relief on farm restructuring; stock relief; accelerated
  capital allowances on qualifying farm buildings/equipment.
- **Leases:** tax-free income thresholds for long-term farm leases
  range from **€18,000 (5-year lease) up to €40,000 (15-year lease)**.
  These income streams are not subject to the standard allowances/caps
  that apply to other income for 2027.
- **Forestry:** annual forestry plantation payments are exempt from
  income tax.
- General closing advice: talk to an accountant/financial adviser
  before making tax-driven decisions — the "right advice is critical."

## 4. "Farming in the future: the skills that will matter" (Claire
Murphy, Irish Country Living Careers & Business — Dr Anne-Marie
Butler, Head of Education, Teagasc)
**Directly relevant to Ambrion/Velocity AI and Meridian's agribusiness
lens.** Key points:
- The future agricultural workforce needs digital awareness, business
  knowledge, adaptability, communication skills, and willingness to
  engage in lifelong learning — traditional practical/technical skills
  remain central but are no longer sufficient alone.
- **On AI specifically — a direct, usable quote:** workers "do not
  necessarily need to become AI experts, but they do need to
  understand how to use it appropriately and critically... those
  entering the industry need to be mindful of what AI will give back.
  You should be able to recognise what's accurate, what's real, and be
  mindful of when you can use AI and when not to. The ability to
  question information, assess its accuracy and understand when
  technology is appropriate will become increasingly important for
  workers." AI is framed as complementing existing agricultural
  knowledge, not replacing it.
- Digital, data and sustainability skills are described as
  "increasingly embedded" across Teagasc courses.
- Business management skills (costs, income, production figures,
  fertiliser requirements, environmental considerations) are
  increasingly integrated into practical learning, not treated as a
  separate subject.
- Practical, hands-on skills remain "fundamental" — described as
  students' consistently favourite modules — alongside apprenticeship
  routes (Teagasc delivers apprenticeships in agriculture, horticulture
  and sports turf management) as an alternative to full-time study.
- Transferable-skills framing: agricultural work builds "a strong work
  ethic, resilience, multitasking skills and the ability to respond to
  unexpected problems," valued by employers even outside agriculture.

## 5. "CAO points surge for veterinary courses as agriculture demand
falls" (Claire Murphy, Irish Country Living Careers & Business)
- First-round CAO 2026 offers: **42% increase** in students selecting
  Level 8 veterinary medicine as first preference (offers up 115%),
  driven partly by two new veterinary medicine courses at SETU and
  ATU. Points: ATU 647, UCD 589, SETU 566 (Level 8). Level 6/7
  veterinary courses saw a 33% rise in first preferences.
- **Agricultural science first preferences fell 7%** at Level 8 (5%
  decline at Level 6/7). Points required for Level 8 agriculture fell
  across the board (UCC highest at 478, down to UCD 417, UG 410, DkIT
  373, SETU 368, ATU 320, TUS 317).
- Professor Frank Buckley (UCC ag science course director) attributes
  the decline partly to normal fluctuation as more course options
  compete for the same student pool, not necessarily a sign agriculture
  itself is less attractive — and flags a genuine "careers perception
  problem": "I don't think students are aware of the degree to which
  there's opportunity within the agricultural industry," citing strong
  employment prospects (research, finance, nutrition, breeding, seed
  companies, agri-tech) for graduates who do enter the sector.
- **Relevant to the talent-pipeline theme already logged** in
  `market_intel_your_future_career_sept2026.md` (engineering shortage,
  skills-over-degree hiring) — agriculture appears to have its own,
  parallel perception/pipeline problem worth being aware of for any
  Meridian agribusiness-sector positioning or Golden Generation
  succession-planning conversation (fewer young entrants choosing
  agricultural science directly affects the future talent pool for
  succession).
