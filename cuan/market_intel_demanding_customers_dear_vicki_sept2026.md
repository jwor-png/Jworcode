# Market Intelligence — "Is holding on to my loyal but demanding
customers worth the extra headaches?" (Dear Vicki, with Vicki O'Toole)

Newspaper clipping photographed by John, 9 Sept 2026, for the
knowledge base. General business-advisory column.

## The question
A small-business owner describes a long-standing, loyal but very
demanding customer who takes up a disproportionate share of the
team's time (constant requests, last-minute changes), requiring the
owner to personally step in to smooth things over. The owner questions
whether the business this customer generates is actually worth the
time/attention cost, while also not wanting to damage a valuable,
long-term relationship. Core question: how do you know when to invest
more in a customer, when to set boundaries, and when it's simply time
to walk away?

## Vicki O'Toole's response — key points
- Rejects "the customer is always right" as a governing principle: the
  customer matters, but that doesn't mean every customer is a **good**
  customer for your specific business.
- **First diagnostic: what is the customer actually worth?** Turnover
  alone is misleading — a customer generating €100,000 in sales but
  demanding constant extras, tying up senior people and huge amounts
  of team time may have a real cost that erodes or exceeds that
  headline revenue figure. The real question is the *net* cost of the
  relationship, not the top-line number.
- **Personal case study, from her own background running JJ O'Toole:**
  she treated every client with the same high-touch, personal
  approach (deep product knowledge, on-the-spot decision-making,
  face-to-face meetings) — a deliberate, effective strategy that won
  business, but it also created an expectation that **she personally**
  would always be the one in the room.
- **Illustrative incident:** on one occasion, snowed under, she sent a
  fully-briefed, highly capable colleague to a routine client meeting.
  The colleague handled it excellently — but the client's CEO still
  called afterwards to politely say he still expected to see Vicki
  personally, despite the colleague's competence.
- **Her own reflection on this:** "the very personal touch that helped
  build our reputation also created a constraint, making me an
  unintended bottleneck. If you are constantly stepping in to smooth
  things over, you may be unintentionally training your clients to
  rely on you rather than respecting your team's role and boundaries."
  — a sharp, directly quotable line on founder-dependency risk.
- **The diagnostic questions she recommends asking before walking away
  from a demanding customer:**
  1. Is the relationship actually profitable?
  2. How much management time does it consume?
  3. Have expectations crept beyond what was originally agreed?
  4. Could clearer boundaries, or charging for additional work, change
     the dynamic?
  5. Could someone else in the business gradually become more
     involved, to reduce single-person dependency?
- **Her conclusion:** if all those avenues have been explored and the
  customer still drains time, frustrates the team, and erodes margin,
  "walking away isn't just an option — it is the right commercial
  decision." Growth isn't simply about adding revenue; it's about
  protecting the business's operational health. **The best client
  relationships are commercially sustainable for both sides, built on
  mutual respect** (the pull-quote used in the layout).
