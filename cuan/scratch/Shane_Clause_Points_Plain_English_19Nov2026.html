Start here: what a constitution is, and what ours should do

What it is: a company's rulebook. It sets how shares work, who gets a vote, who appoints and removes directors, how decisions are made and what directors may do. It is filed at the Companies Registration Office, so investors, banks and lawyers can read it and rely on it. The Companies Act sits above it: where they clash, the law wins. It is hard to change (a 75% special resolution and a new filing, and once other people hold shares, their class must agree too). So it holds permanent rules. Commercial detail that may change, such as investor rights and prices, sits in private contracts alongside it, like the shareholders' agreement.

What ours should reflect, for an AI company with a significant degree of flexibility:

1. Your control: A shares carry the votes, so you direct the group.
2. Flexibility: new companies, share classes and employee participation can be added quickly, with no capital ceiling.
3. Protected IP and subsidiaries: the crown jewels stay inside the group.
4. Clarity for investors: equal economics per share, a fair exit offer, and no doubt about who decides what. Clear wording removes uncertainty.
5. Continuity: the company can still act if you are unavailable.
6. Lawful and consistent: nothing that clashes with the Act, and it matches the term sheet.

A simple test for every clause below: (a) does it say what you intend, in one reading? (b) would an investor's lawyer read it the same way? (c) does it still work if you are unavailable? (d) is it a permanent rule for the constitution, or a deal term for the shareholders' agreement? (e) can Asterial and the other subsidiaries copy it?

1. Your definition of "group" (Regulation 5.2)

What it does: it just says "group" means Noesis and the companies it owns. Our view: fine, no change.

2. Clause 13.2: you can run other businesses

What it does: the law says directors must avoid conflicts of interest. This clause says a director who holds A shares (you) is allowed to have other businesses outside the group and is let off that duty to the extent needed.

Our suggestion: add one sentence saying this does not remove other duties, such as telling the board about a personal interest in a deal, and keeping the company's confidential information confidential.

Why: some of those duties apply by law anyway, so the sentence costs you nothing. It stops an investor's lawyer arguing the clause is wider than you meant, and it shows you have thought about it.

3. Clause 13.3: group property and IP stay in the group

What it does: nobody may take the group's property, confidential information or IP outside the group without "the Company's agreement".

Our suggestion: say who gives that agreement: written approval from the directors other than the person asking. For IP, also your A consent under Regulation 28.

Why: at present you are the only director. As written, you could give the company's agreement to yourself. That looks like no protection at all to an investor. A second signature makes the protection real, and it protects you if anyone later questions a decision.

4. Optional new 13.4: tell the secretary about competing ventures

What it does: a director gives written notice of any outside venture that competes with a group business, and the secretary keeps a register.

Why: your clause lets you run "any" business, which in theory includes a competitor. Investors and banks will notice. A simple notice shows nothing is hidden and costs one email. It is optional: your call.

5. Regulation 28: your consent for big decisions

What it does: the company cannot sell shares in a subsidiary or sell, charge or exclusively license its IP without your A consent. In plain terms, nobody sells the crown jewels without you.

Three changes we suggest:

(a) Ordinary customer licences are allowed. Add: a standard non-exclusive licence to a customer, in the ordinary course, does not need your consent. Why: otherwise every customer deal needs your signature, which slows the company down just when you want speed.

(b) Issuing new shares in a subsidiary also needs your consent. Why: selling existing shares is covered, but creating new ones for an investor, which dilutes the company's holding, is not clearly covered.

(c) Every subsidiary needs the same clause, starting with Asterial. Why: Regulation 28 binds Noesis only. The IP is moving into Asterial next week, and Asterial's own board could sell it without any vote by Noesis. Unless Asterial's constitution says the same, the protection has a hole exactly where the IP will sit.
