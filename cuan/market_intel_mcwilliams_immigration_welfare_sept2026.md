# "To take in migrants we must change our welfare system" — David McWilliams

Source: Irish Times Weekend, 26 Sept 2026. **Same columnist already tracked** for the housing-turn piece (`market_intel_mcwilliams_housing_turn_oct2026.md`) — second McWilliams piece now on file.

## What it says

**The numbers:** Irish population rose 66,900 last year, to 5,525,600 — the highest level since the Famine. 110,600 immigrants arrived, 62,500 emigrants left (net migration +48,100); natural increase (births minus deaths) was 18,800 — meaning **immigration accounted for 72% of the population increase last year.**

**Core argument:** most political debate on immigration is rhetorical (entrenched "always too much" vs "never enough" camps), so McWilliams argues for being guided by emerging granular fiscal research rather than the loudest voices. Cites a Financial Times piece ("Unpacking the real fiscal costs of immigration") drawing on a new Stanford working paper (Usama Polani) building a 60-year life-cycle fiscal model for the UK, Germany, France and Spain.

**The key distinction the research makes:** fiscal outcome depends less on *who* migrants are and more on *which tax/welfare system* they arrive into. A country with a progressive tax system (low earners pay little tax, like Ireland) and a **flat, universal state pension** (not contribution-linked) sees immigrants — especially low-wage ones — take out more over a lifetime than they put in, structurally.

**Comparative breakeven earnings needed to "pay your way" over a lifetime** (average immigrant couple arriving at 30): France ~45th earnings percentile, Germany ~28th, UK ~55th (or: avg couple needs the main earner on the 65th percentile, ~€62,000+/year, per McWilliams' own Irish estimate below). The UK's own flatter system makes the bar lower than Ireland's.

**McWilliams' own "back of the envelope" Irish estimate** (explicitly flagged as **indicative, not precise**): for an average immigrant couple arriving at 30, the main breadwinner needs to be in the **65th earnings percentile (~€62,000+/year)** to pay their way over a lifetime. For migrants from "female seclusion" societies (his term, drawn from the Stanford model — low female paid-employment rates, associated with parts of Central/South Asia, the Middle East, North Africa) where the man is typically the sole earner, that figure rises to the **85th percentile (~€95,000+/year)** for the couple to break even.

**Why Ireland's system makes this worse, structurally, not about who's arriving:**
- Progressive tax + flat universal pension combination: a worker on half the average salary (€27,000) receives ~€11,151/year in net state support; an average earner (€52,200) pays €8,294/year into the system; double-average (€104,000) pays €40,362.
- **The flat state pension specifically**: someone on minimum wage for 40 years, paying no tax, receives the same €299.30/week pension as someone who earned €150,000/year for 40 years and paid enormous tax — pension is "unrelated to the amount of tax paid."

**McWilliams' conclusion:** unless Ireland changes its tax and welfare system, the country can really only afford highly-skilled immigration on a sustainable fiscal basis. To take in large numbers of low-skilled migrants without structural reform would require genuine welfare-system change — he flags this as a candidate for "the big policy debate of the next decade."
