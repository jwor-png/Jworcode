# "The USC was meant to be a temporary, emergency tax. Why do we still have it?" — Irish Independent, 9 Oct 2026

Opinion column, Martina Devlin, published the day after Budget 2027.

## Key points

- **USC (Universal Social Charge)** introduced January 2011 as Ireland entered the EU-IMF bailout programme, replacing two existing levies (health and income). Explicitly framed as a temporary emergency/recession measure at the time.
- **13 years after exiting the bailout, it's still here.** Budget 2027 made minor tinkering only — taoiseach-in-waiting **Simon Harris dismissed winding it down or abolishing it**, despite having called USC a "hated" tax 10 years ago and pledged to phase it out.
- **Current structure:** anyone earning over €13,000 pays USC on full income, progressive rates 0.5%–8%, top rate applying to earnings above €70,044 — in addition to PAYE and PRSI.
- **A long pattern of broken promises across both main parties:** 2016 Enda Kenny/Michael Noonan pledged a roadmap to eliminate USC by 2020/2021; the 2016 Fianna Fáil manifesto promised removal for low/middle earners up to €80,000; Micheál Martin later attacked Fine Gael for "false promises" on abolition while admitting Fianna Fáil wouldn't abandon it either.
- **Revenue raised:** generates **€5bn+ annually** for the Exchequer — Department of Finance is "particularly attached to it" as a reliable, efficient income stream, an antidote to volatile corporation tax receipts. Total net tax receipts collected by the Exchequer were **€106.5bn in 2025** (May 2026 Revenue report).
- **Harris's own quoted justification** (RTÉ radio phone-in, day after the budget): the Government "relied on it and admitted it was too useful to abandon."
- **Devlin's three-point framing for whether to retain it:**
  1. Irish income tax is high vs. other EU countries (Ireland ranks 6th highest of EU-27 for a single person without children, per Eurostat 2025) — though the "tax wedge" (employer cost vs. employee take-home) tells a better story: Ireland's effective tax wedge last year was 22.5% vs. a 39.5% EU average, lowest in the EU, due to lower social-security-contribution levels here vs. elsewhere.
  2. State finances are healthy — risks exist (heavy reliance on foreign-owned multinational tax receipts) but Ireland is "in good shape overall."
  3. Continuing an "austerity levy" at current levels, long after the crisis that justified it, is her own view "unjust" — frames continued retention as "more about wasteful use of the overall tax take than the USC's importance."
- **Cost-of-living counterpoint:** USC adds to an already-stretched household position — high energy bills, VAT at 23% (above EU average; Germany 19%, France/Austria 20%, Belgium/Spain/Netherlands 21%), motor tax plus toll costs, people effectively paying twice for healthcare (PAYE/USC funding public health system plus private health insurance to cover gaps).
- **Possible reform options raised but not chosen by Government:** a phased reduction, raising the entry point significantly, reducing rates, or exempting incomes below €46,305 (the CSO's median annual earnings figure).
