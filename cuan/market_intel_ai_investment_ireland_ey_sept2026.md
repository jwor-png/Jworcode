# Market Intelligence — "AI investment isn't just about chasing
returns — future prosperity and security are at stake" (Grit Young,
Opinion/Business)

Newspaper clipping photographed by John, 9 Sept 2026. Author: **Grit
Young, EY Ireland Partner and TMT (Technology, Media & Telecoms)
Leader.** Logged for the knowledge base per John's instruction.

## Core argument
The two-year debate about whether AI investment is "a bubble" is, per
the author, the wrong question. AI infrastructure spending is
projected to reach **almost $2.9tn (€2.5tn) between 2025 and 2028** —
so the more useful question isn't whether the spending will all pay
off in traditional ROI terms, but **whether companies and countries
can afford not to invest.**

## Why traditional ROI framing doesn't fit
- Assessing every euro of AI spend through a conventional
  return-on-investment lens made sense when technology investment was
  primarily about efficiency, growth or productivity. It makes less
  sense for technology that is "rapidly becoming part of the
  infrastructure that underpins economies and societies."
- **Railway boom analogy:** like 19th-century rail investment, huge
  capital is being committed to infrastructure before anyone knows
  exactly where demand will settle.
- **Key distinction from prior tech booms:** governments and businesses
  aren't simply buying computer capacity — they're buying **resilience,
  strategic autonomy, and a degree of insurance** against an
  increasingly uncertain world. That reframes AI infrastructure as a
  **strategic asset**, not a discretionary investment.
- This doesn't mean every investment will succeed — there will be
  winners and losers, underused infrastructure, and companies that
  fail to capture the returns they expected. That doesn't automatically
  make the investment irrational, given what's actually being bought
  (strategic security, not just compute).
- **The real executive question today, per the author:** what happens
  if access to critical AI capabilities becomes constrained,
  regulatory frameworks diverge across jurisdictions, geopolitical
  tensions intensify, tokens become too expensive, or the underlying
  technologies a business depends on sit largely outside its control?
  This is presented as the actual driver of continued heavy investment
  despite economic uncertainty, higher interest rates, and growing
  regulation — not investors ignoring the risk, but pricing in a
  different kind of risk (strategic dependency) than the traditional
  ROI model captures.

## Europe and Ireland specifically
- For Europe, AI ambition is often framed as "a race to catch up," but
  the author argues the real goal isn't producing the next AI champion
  — it's **ensuring Europe retains meaningful influence over
  technologies that will shape the decades ahead.**
- Regulation, infrastructure, investment and talent "cannot be viewed
  in isolation" — they're all part of the same competitiveness
  conversation. The real European challenge isn't whether to regulate
  AI, but ensuring regulation is matched with investment, capability
  and ambition.
- **On Ireland specifically — the most directly usable section:**
  Irish debate has understandably focused on data centres, electricity
  demand and infrastructure capacity, but the author argues **the
  highest-value opportunity for Ireland may not be hosting every
  server.** Instead, it may be in becoming **the European location
  with recognised expertise in capital allocation, risk management,
  regulation, energy planning, corporate finance and technology
  governance** for the AI economy. Ireland is described as already
  having "significant strengths" in these areas — potentially "one of
  the most important investment stories of decades to come."
- Direct quote worth keeping: "The future AI economy will be shaped by
  those who decide where it is built, how it is financed and who gets
  access to it."
