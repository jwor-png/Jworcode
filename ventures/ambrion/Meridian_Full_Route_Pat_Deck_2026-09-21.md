# PART A — CHALLENGING THE SECOND CRITIQUE

The Fast mode critique is **better than the first one.** It is more disciplined about what it did not check, it tags its own confidence, and it caught four things both Jürgen and Meridian missed. It also contains three points that do not survive.

## A1. WHERE IT IS RIGHT AND BOTH EARLIER REVIEWS WERE WRONG

**The AI literacy point is correct, and it corrects Meridian.** Meridian's first pass called AI literacy the biggest gap in the deck and described Article 4 as if unchanged. **The Digital Omnibus rewrote Article 4 in full.** The duty moved from taking measures to *ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level* of AI literacy, to taking measures to *support the development* of AI literacy, taking account of technical knowledge, experience, education, training, the context of use and the persons on whom the systems are used. **It is now an obligation of effort rather than result, and it expressly does not require any specific level to be guaranteed for any individual.**

**Consequences, and Meridian takes the correction.**
- **Meridian's finding survives but weakens.** The duty still binds every provider and deployer in that room today with no threshold, so the gap is real. But it is a softer duty than Meridian implied and must not be sold as "ensure a sufficient level".
- **It creates a better criticism of the deck than the one Meridian made.** Slide 15 says "Prohibited practices and AI literacy since Feb 2025". **That is now stale, in a deck that elsewhere carries the Omnibus correctly.** The deck is inconsistent with itself on the same Regulation.
- **This is the third internal Omnibus inconsistency**, alongside the regulations box and the machinery row below.

**The emotion and fatigue point is a good catch and it is the sharpest legal point in any of the three reviews.** Slide 32 says "Check safety monitoring products for emotion or fatigue detection – banned!" and then, two bullets later, "Certain workplace emotion-recognition uses are prohibited, subject to the AI Act's medical or safety exception." **The slide contradicts itself within four lines**, the exclamation mark overstates, and fatigue detection on a construction site is the exact use case the safety exception exists for. Neither Jürgen nor Meridian saw this. Credit it.

**The Digital Omnibus contradiction is real.** Slide 14 lists "EU Digital Omnibus" under EU REGULATIONS, "Apply directly. No Irish statute required." Slide 16 correctly shows the data and cyber strand as COM(2025) 837, "PROPOSED, still in negotiation". **Only the AI strand is law.** Missed by both earlier reviews.

**The provider versus deployer point is better framed than Meridian's Article 25 point.** Meridian identified the deployer-to-provider trap. The critique identifies the prior and bigger problem: **the deck never tells a construction firm which obligations attach to a deployer at all**, which is the first question anyone in that room had. That is the superior framing and it subsumes the Article 25 point as a sub-case.

**The unsupported statistics are fair.** BoardPro's 79, 44 and 2 per cent carry no sample size or geography, and the two quotations shown are from Australia and New Zealand, presented to an Irish audience. Workiva's one in four has no base. The MIT Delphi chart is unreadable at slide size and carries no stated takeaway. **Three of the four data slides in the deck cannot be relied on, in a deck about verification.**

**The unpaid hook is a genuine editorial catch.** Slide 5, "One person and 54 agents: how do you govern this?", is the strongest image in the deck and is never answered. **Slide 8, Human Gates, is the answer.** Linking them costs nothing and would materially improve the talk.

## A2. WHERE IT OVERREACHES, AND THESE SHOULD NOT BE SENT

**The Irish Examiner date is not "the worst possible mistake".** Both reviews inflate it. The deck is marked **V5R2**, which is revision two of version five, so it has been revised at least twice. **The overwhelmingly likely explanation is a post-conference revision for circulation, with the footer correctly left showing the event date.** The footer is not wrong: 14 September is the event. Only the version block is arguably stale. **It is a housekeeping slip worth mentioning in one line, not a credibility indictment.** Leading a note with it, as both reviews propose, is disproportionate and will read as petty.

**Point 14, "32 items and no clear first step", is overstated.** Slide 6 is numbered one to six and item one is where to start. **Slide 32 is explicitly built as a first step: "Different parts of the industry are on different clocks. Find yours, and start with the first date that applies to you."** The deck gives a first step twice. The fair criticism is that the lists are never consolidated, not that the audience leaves with nothing.

**Point 12, the UK and US balance, is asserted without testing.** Four slides of thirty-four is under twelve per cent. **Irish construction firms work in the UK routinely, and the UK section carries the most transferable board content in the deck**, including the line that using AI does not transfer accountability to the model, vendor or IT function. **The critique treats geography as self-evidently irrelevant. It is not, and Pat will say so.** The defensible version is that the US section is the one that could be halved.

**Point 17, the disclaimer, is the weakest point in the critique.** Describing a prohibited practice as banned is stating the law, not advising on it. **Every conference speaker in the country does this.** The real problem with that slide is the internal contradiction identified at A1, not the disclaimer's reach.

**And a house-style item that must be stripped before anything is sent.** The Polish list ends with "em dashes throughout". **Em dashes are standard typography and their absence is John's own writing rule, not a defect in anyone else's document.** Sending it as a criticism exports a personal preference as a professional standard and would be noticed.

# PART B — THE FULL SEVEN-DOMAIN ROUTE

## 1. COMMERCIAL AND DEAL — THE FINDING NOBODY HAS MADE

**The deck names fifteen National Competent Authorities, thirteen Market Surveillance Authorities, nine Article 77 authorities and the AI Office of Ireland. For an Irish construction firm, none of those is the mechanism that will actually bind it.**

**The binding mechanism is the contract.**

Pat is half way there and does not generalise it. Slide 14: *"NIS2-aligned requirements are already being imposed on many Irish organisations through supply chain contracts."* Slide 33: *"NIS2 clauses are already arriving in contracts from energy, water, transport and health clients."*

**That insight is the most commercially important thing in the deck and it is buried in two footnotes.** A subcontractor will not first meet the AI Act through a Market Surveillance Authority. It will meet it as a clause in a main contract, a collateral warranty, a framework agreement or a PQQ, imposed by a client who is itself regulated, months or years before any regulator calls.

**The V6 slide that does not exist: how this reaches you.** Through your client's contract, through your PI policy wording, through procurement prequalification, and only then through a regulator. **For a construction audience that is the whole game**, and it converts a compliance calendar into something a commercial director can act on this week.

**Also missing: nothing in the deck addresses what to do when a client's AI clause is unreasonable.** Flow-down, caps, and who carries the risk of a vendor's non-compliance are live commercial questions today.

## 2. LEGAL AND GOVERNANCE — AS SET OUT IN THE FIRST PASS, WITH ONE ADDITION

Covered at length in the 21 September pass: section 228(1)(g) absent, Caremark correctly placed, the Product Liability charge unfounded, Article 25 not applied to the AI Act.

**One addition on challenge.** Both reviews say the Irish director duty is missing and both are right. **Neither notices that the deck does carry an Irish personal liability hook and buries it.** Slide 20, the NCSC Guidance slide, quotes NIS2 Article 20: management bodies must approve cybersecurity risk-management measures, oversee implementation, **"and can be held liable for infringements"**. Slide 21 adds the National Cyber Security Bill's Head 28, "Management Board approval and oversight of cyber risk measures, **with personal liability**", and draft penalties to €10m or two per cent of turnover.

**So the deck has personal liability, in the cyber frame, with a number attached, and never connects it to AI governance or to company law.** That is a stronger and more constructive observation than "section 228 is missing", because it points at material Pat already has.

## 3. FINANCE AND RESTRUCTURING

**The deck has no cost content of any kind.** Thirty-four substantive slides on obligations, and not one line on what any of it costs.

For a construction audience on thin margins, the unanswered questions are: what does an AI inventory cost to build and maintain, what does third-party conformity assessment under the Machinery Regulation cost and how long is the notified body queue, what does ISO 42001 certification cost, and what happens to a PI premium once AI-assisted design is disclosed.

**Credit where both critiques failed to give it.** Pat has the insurance point twice and it is good. Slide 6 item 6: *"Talk to your PI broker/insurer regarding your cover for AI-related designs/products."* Slide 32: *"Ask your insurer, in writing, whether the PI policy covers AI-assisted work."* **In writing is exactly right, and neither reviewer acknowledged it.** Any note that lists seventeen faults without crediting that will read as unbalanced.

**The gap is that the insurance point is advice without consequence.** What does a firm do if the answer is no. That is the finance question and the deck does not ask it.

**Slide 33 carries a real commercial trap that gets one line:** *"Notified body capacity is finite and January 2027 is fixed."* **That is a scheduling and cash-flow problem disguised as a compliance point**, and for the make-or-modify audience it is the most urgent thing on the slide.

## 4. PROPERTY AND DEVELOPMENT — NOT A NIL RETURN, AND THIS IS WHERE THE UNCLOSED GAP SITS

Both reviews flag the missing Building Control anchor and both correctly decline to cite it. **Meridian cannot close it either and will not assert it.** But the domain can say what shape the question takes, which neither review does.

**The exposure is not the AI Act. It is that Irish building control runs on named individuals signing personal certificates.** An assigned certifier and a design certifier put their names to statutory certificates of compliance. **The question for V6 is not whether AI is regulated. It is whether a named certifier can certify work they did not personally produce, and what their professional indemnity insurer thinks about that.**

**That is the sharpest sector-specific question available and it requires no AI Act at all.** It exists today, it is personal, and it is exactly the "liability that exists now" both reviews grope towards.

**Credit again.** Slide 32 already says *"Keep certification and sign-off with named people, and record that the check happened."* **Pat has the instinct. He has not named the regime that makes it a statutory matter rather than good practice.**

**Also in this domain and missed by everyone:** slide 33's Construction Products Regulation card is genuinely good and sector-specific, including the Digital Product Passport intended to interoperate with BIM. **Neither critique credits it, and point 11's complaint that the BIM mandate is "not AI law" ignores that slide 33 gives the legal basis on which BIM and product data are converging.**

## 5. AI STRATEGY AND ADOPTION

**The deck is entirely about obligation and contains no adoption content.** For a conference titled *Beyond the prompt: AI for the Sustainable Built Environment*, delivered to a High Performance Building Alliance, the governance talk never touches what the technology is for.

That is a defensible choice for a governance slot. **But there is no bridge, and the audience is left with the impression that AI is a liability to be managed rather than a capability to be governed.** One slide saying what good adoption looks like under this framework would change the tone of the whole talk.

**The AI Regulatory Sandbox is listed on slide 27 and never explained.** Under Article 57, now under the AI Office of Ireland. **For a construction firm wanting to deploy site monitoring before December 2027, the sandbox is the single most practically useful thing on that slide, and it gets one line.**

**And the deployer question again, from this domain's angle.** Most of that room deploys vendor tools. They need to know they must use systems in line with the provider's instructions and assign human oversight to people with the competence, authority and support to exercise it. **Slide 8's Human Gates is that, presented as Pat's recommendation rather than as a duty with a date.** Cross-referencing it would convert the best slide in the deck from advice into law.

## 6. BUSINESS TRANSFORMATION AND GROWTH

**The consolidation problem, stated more usefully than "32 items".** The deck contains six board recommendations, twelve director questions, five human gates, four construction board questions and the NCSC's five. **They overlap heavily and are never mapped onto each other.**

**The V6 fix is one page, not a rewrite:** a single consolidated checklist showing which of those items is a legal duty with a date, which is guidance a regulator will measure you against, and which is Pat's own recommendation. **That one distinction is the entire difference between a compliance calendar and a governance framework**, and it answers the structural criticism both earlier reviews made badly.

**Sequencing.** Slide 32 is the best slide in the deck because it segments by role and gives each segment one date. **It appears at slide 32 of 43.** Moving it forward would let everything after it hang off a structure the audience already has.

## 7. AI EQUITY AND INVESTMENT — THIN BUT NOT NIL

*Frontier caveat applies: professional judgement, not established practice.*

**Slide 37 carries "AI washing" as a US securities matter.** The SEC's Cyber and Emerging Technologies Unit treating overstated AI claims as an enforcement priority.

**The Irish read that is missing:** overstated AI capability in an information memorandum, a PQQ response, a grant application or a set of accounts is a misrepresentation risk here too, without any AI statute. **For any firm in that room heading for a sale, a refinancing or an Enterprise Ireland application, the exposure is the claim, not the technology.**

One line. It would land hard with owner-managers and it costs nothing.
