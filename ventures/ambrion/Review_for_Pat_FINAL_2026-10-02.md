# REVIEW: GOVERNANCE OF AI

**Deck:** Pat McGrath, McGrath Strategia, V5R2, 14 September 2026
**Event:** Beyond the Prompt, AI for the Sustainable Built Environment. Construction Cluster Ireland and the High Performance Building Alliance, TU Dublin
**Reviewed by:** John Webb O'Rourke
**Date:** 2 October 2026
**Status:** Working notes offered for V6. Not legal advice. The verification record at the end sets out what was retrieved from source and what was not.

---

## 1. HEADLINE

This is a serious and current piece of work, and the volume of material you have assembled on the Irish position is better than anything else I have seen on the circuit. Everything below is offered on that basis.

Two things would make V6 materially stronger. The first is that the Irish board is the only one of the three jurisdictions you cover that is given regulators rather than personal exposure. The second is that the governance material in slides 6 to 8 is the best content in the deck and never returns as a spine for the thirty slides that follow it.

---

## 2. THE IRISH DIRECTOR GAP, AND IT IS THE BIGGEST OPPORTUNITY IN THE DECK

Look at what each jurisdiction gets.

The US board gets a precise fiduciary anchor: Caremark, the 2023 McDonald's decision, and the line that the question a court asks is not whether the model was correct but what system the board put in place to know.

The UK board gets SM&CR, Consumer Duty, the Mills Review, and the observation that using AI does not transfer accountability to the model, vendor or IT function.

The Irish board gets fifteen National Competent Authorities, thirteen Market Surveillance Authorities, nine Article 77 bodies, the AI Office of Ireland and S.I. 366 of 2025. It is comprehensive on who regulates. It says nothing about what an Irish director personally owes.

**You already hold the Irish personal liability material and do not use it.** Slide 20 quotes NIS2 Article 20, that management bodies approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures, oversee implementation and "can be held liable for infringements". Slide 21 adds Head 28 of the National Cyber Security Bill, "Management Board approval and oversight of cyber risk measures, with personal liability", and draft penalties up to €10m or two per cent of turnover.

**That thread is sitting in your cyber section and is never joined to AI governance.** Connecting the two would give the Irish part of the talk the anchor the other two jurisdictions already have, using material you have already researched.

**A second option, offered for you to form your own view on.** Section 228(1)(g) of the Companies Act 2014 requires a director to exercise the care, skill and diligence that would be exercised in the same circumstances by a reasonable person having both the knowledge and experience that may reasonably be expected of a person in the same position as the director, and the knowledge and experience that the director in fact has.

The second limb is interesting for a speaker, because it is at least arguable that a director who has been briefed on AI risk is thereafter measured against what they now know. **I would be careful with it.** It is an assertion about personal legal exposure, made to a room of directors by someone who is not their solicitor, in a session you have expressly disclaimed as not legal advice. It is a strong line and it carries more exposure than anything currently in the deck. Your call entirely, and worth taking your own advice on before using it.

## 3. PROVIDER VERSUS DEPLOYER, WHICH IS THE COMMERCIAL POINT

Most of the firms in that room are deployers of AI, not providers. The deck never draws the distinction, so nobody leaves knowing which obligations attach to them.

It also misses the trap that matters most commercially in this sector. On secondary sources, a deployer takes on the full provider obligation set by putting its own name or trademark on a high-risk system, by making a substantial modification to it, or by changing its intended purpose so that it becomes high-risk.

**You raise substantial modification correctly for machinery on slide 33 and never apply it to the AI Act itself.** White-labelling a vendor's tool is routine for main contractors, and that one bullet would land harder than anything else on the slide.

---

## 4. THREE INTERNAL INCONSISTENCIES, OFFERED AS ONE ITEM

**The Digital Omnibus appears on both sides of the line.** The regulations box lists "EU Digital Omnibus" among instruments that apply directly with no Irish statute required. A later slide correctly shows the data and cyber strand as COM(2025) 837, still a proposal in negotiation. Only the AI strand is law.

**The AI literacy line is out of date.** The deck states that prohibited practices and AI literacy have applied since February 2025 and leaves it there. The Omnibus changed the AI literacy duty with effect from 27 July 2026. Providers and deployers must now take measures that support the development of AI literacy, taking account of knowledge, experience, education, training, context of use and the persons affected, rather than guaranteeing a specific level.

**The emotion detection bullet contradicts itself four lines later.** "Check safety monitoring products for emotion or fatigue detection, banned!" sits two bullets above "Certain workplace emotion-recognition uses are prohibited, subject to the AI Act's medical or safety exception." Fatigue is a physical state rather than necessarily emotion recognition, and the safety exception is exactly what a site monitoring tool would rely on. Pick one.

---

## 5. THE MACHINERY ROW IS THE MOST PRACTICAL SLIDE AND THE LEAST CLEAR

Under the Machinery Regulation heading the deck says "The Omnibus moved the application date for Annex I to 2 Aug 2028", which reads as though the Machinery Regulation's own date had moved. It has not. The AI Act's Annex I obligations moved to 2 August 2028. The Machinery Regulation still applies from 20 January 2027.

Slides 32 and 33 both get this right and carry 20 January 2027 for the make-or-modify audience, so it is a wording problem on one card rather than an error of substance. It is worth fixing because that audience has the nearest deadline.

The Omnibus does also amend the Machinery Regulation and the deck never says what that amendment was. I have not retrieved it and have not asserted anything about it.

## 6. WHERE THE AUDIENCE ACTUALLY MEETS THIS, AND IT IS NOT A REGULATOR

This is the one suggestion that is an addition rather than a correction.

The deck names fifteen competent authorities, thirteen market surveillance authorities and nine fundamental rights bodies. For a main contractor or a design practice, **none of those is the mechanism that will bind them first. The contract is.**

You are halfway there already and it sits in two footnotes. One says NIS2-aligned requirements are already being imposed on Irish organisations through supply chain contracts. The other says NIS2 clauses are already arriving in contracts from energy, water, transport and health clients.

**That insight is the most commercially useful thing in the deck and it is buried.** A subcontractor will meet the AI Act as a clause in a main contract, a collateral warranty, a framework agreement or a prequalification questionnaire, imposed by a client who is itself regulated, long before any regulator makes contact. A slide on how this reaches you, through your client's contract, your PI policy wording and procurement, then eventually a regulator, would convert the compliance calendar into something a commercial director can act on this week.

## 7. STRUCTURE AND AUDIENCE

**The governance content never returns as a spine.** Slide 4 opens on Cadbury, governance as the system by which companies are directed and controlled. Slides 6, 7 and 8 then deliver exactly that: six board recommendations, twelve director questions and five human gates. They are the strongest material in the deck.

They then stop, and thirty slides of regulatory map follow beside them rather than hanging off them. Would V6 be stronger if the instruments were organised against those three slides rather than presented in parallel to them?

**The lists are never consolidated.** Six board recommendations, twelve director questions, five human gates, four construction board questions and the NCSC's five. They overlap heavily and nothing is ranked. One page showing which items are a legal duty with a date, which are guidance a regulator will measure against, and which are your own recommendation, would be the single highest-value addition to V6. That distinction is the whole difference between a compliance calendar and a governance framework.

**The best hook is never paid off.** "One person and 54 agents, how do you govern this?" is the strongest image in the deck and is never answered. The human gates slide is the answer. Linking them explicitly costs nothing.

**The balance could shift.** Four slides on UK and US law is defensible given how many Irish firms work in Britain, and the UK material carries genuinely transferable board content. The US section is the one that could halve, and the time saved belongs with deployer duties and the Irish director position.

**There is no close.** The deck ends on a standards chart. No summary, no three things to do on Monday, no contact slide.

**The supporting statistics will not bear weight.** The BoardPro figures of 79, 44 and 2 per cent carry no sample size or geography and the quotes shown are from Australia and New Zealand. The Workiva one in four has no base. The MIT Delphi chart cannot be read at slide size and carries no stated takeaway.

**And the BIM mandate is procurement policy rather than AI law.** Leading the "AI law and the construction industry" slide with it blurs the category and invites a challenge from the floor.

## 8. A SHARPER NIS2 POINT WAS AVAILABLE

You correctly note the referral of Ireland to the Court of Justice on 8 July 2026. What gives it teeth is omitted: the Commission asked the Court to impose a lump sum and daily penalty payments running until transposition is complete, and Ireland was referred alongside Spain, France and the Netherlands.

## 9. POLISH

Each of these costs a little credibility in a talk about verification.

Slide 3 carries an Irish Examiner quotation dated 18 September 2026 while the deck is labelled V5R2 14Sep26 and every footer reads TUD, 14 September 2026. I assume the deck was updated between outings, which is the right instinct, but the version block and footers should move with it.

NIST is the National Institute of Standards and Technology, not Science and Technology.

The slide caption describing the AI Office of Ireland as established under the "AI Regulation Bill 2026" copies the Department's own headline, but the Act was signed on 21 July and it should be corrected.

Slide 32's line "From Dec 2026, the Product Liability Directive treats software, including AI systems, as products" would be tighter as "for products placed on the market after that date". Slide 33 already covers the continuing-control position with "no escape where the defect stems from failure to supply updates within your control", so this is a summary-slide tidy rather than a substantive point.

Broken headings "EURO PEA N UNION" and "IRE LA ND". "SNote" on the transposition slide. A stray "8" on the human gates slide. A stray closing quotation mark on the FCA slide. A missing full stop in the Machinery paragraph, "will not do A substantial". A trailing double full stop on the NIS2 supply chain line.

## 10. WHAT HOLDS UP

The core dates are sound and I checked them.

The AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. Annex III high-risk obligations apply from 2 December 2027 and Annex I from 2 August 2028. Article 50 transparency, the prohibitions and the GPAI duties were not deferred.

The Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Act 2026, No. 31 of 2026, was signed on 21 July 2026 and commenced on 31 July 2026 other than two paragraphs of the definition in section 78, so "commenced in part" is accurate.

The Product Liability Directive references are correct. Transposition is due by 9 December 2026, which is what the deck says throughout.

Ireland was referred to the Court of Justice over NIS2 on 8 July 2026.

The NCSC published its Guidance on Cyber Governance for Management Board Members in NIS2 Entities on 7 July 2026.

## 11. FIVE OPEN ITEMS I DID NOT CHECK

1. Whether the Irish Examiner quotation of 18 September 2026 exists as described.
2. What the Hugging Face cyber incident refers to. The slide teases it and never explains it, and it is the kind of thing that gets asked in Q&A.
3. The exact amendment the Omnibus made to the Machinery Regulation.
4. Building Control certification and health and safety duties as they apply to AI-assisted work. Where does AI-assisted design sit against sign-off by an assigned or ancillary certifier, and against the site monitoring duties that apply today? Both create personal exposure now rather than in 2027.
5. Whether the list of fifteen National Competent Authorities and nine Article 77 bodies matches S.I. 366 of 2025 as amended.
