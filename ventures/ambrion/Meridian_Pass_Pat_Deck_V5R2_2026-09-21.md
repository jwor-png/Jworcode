# PART ONE — WHAT BREAKS

## BREAK 1: THE PRODUCT LIABILITY DATE CHARGE IS UNFOUNDED

**This is the most important finding in the pass.**

Jürgen's charge: the deck says the PLD applies from 9 December 2026, when a May 2026 corrigendum changed Article 2(1) to 8 December 2026.

**Every single PLD reference in the deck is to the transposition deadline, not the application date.** There are four:

| Slide | Exact wording |
|---|---|
| 15, EU 1 of 3 | "Member State transposition **due** 9 Dec 2026. Ireland – when?" |
| 18, sequencing timeline | "Member States **must have transposed** the recast Product Liability Directive" |
| 21, Ireland 2 of 3 | "Product Liability Directive **due** 9 Dec 2026 — around three months away at date of this conference" |
| 33, Where it actually bites | "Ireland **must transpose** the new legal requirement by 9 Dec 2026" |

**The corrigendum changed Article 2(1), which is the application cut-off for products placed on the market. It did not change the transposition deadline, which remains 9 December 2026.** Those are two different dates doing two different jobs in the same instrument.

**Pat never states an application date anywhere in the deck.** He therefore cannot have got it wrong.

**If John opens the note with this, he tells Pat that a correct statement is an error, using a corrigendum that does not touch the statement made.** Pat would check it in five minutes, find he is right, and every remaining point in the note would be read through that failure. **This point must be removed entirely, not softened.**

**What survives, and it is worth keeping as a refinement rather than a correction.** Slide 32 tells the make-or-modify audience: "From Dec 2026, the Product Liability Directive treats software, including AI systems, as products." A room full of installed plant could read that as catching legacy equipment. The Directive reaches products placed on the market after the cut-off. **That is a useful sentence to add to V6, not a misstatement to correct.** Slide 33 partly covers it already with "No escape where the defect stems from failure to supply updates within your control."

## BREAK 2: THE STRUCTURAL CHARGE IS WRONG ON THE FACE OF THE DECK

Jürgen: "Slide 4 opens with Cadbury. Nothing after it applies that definition. What follows is a regulatory catalogue."

**The three slides immediately after Cadbury are pure governance.**

- **Slide 6, The Board and AI.** Six recommendations: governance systems and policies, **clear executive accountability**, an AI inventory with material risk classification, board competence and training, regular review of AI activity and assurance, and PI cover. Closing line: *"Governance starts with verification, evidence and human accountability."*
- **Slide 7, Questions for Directors.** Twelve, including *"Can we prove we comply?"*, *"Can we trace everything back to its source?"*, *"Is the governance process actually being followed?"* and *"Who carries responsibility and legal liability?"*
- **Slide 8, Human Gates.** Five named approval points where only a human may approve: legal interpretation, design of automated controls, risk acceptance, deployment, regulatory attestation.

**That is who decides, on what evidence, with what record.** It is exactly what the note says is absent.

**It recurs at the end too.** Slide 32 carries four Questions for the Board. Slides 34 to 37 each close with a "For the board" box, and slide 33's is the best line in the deck: *"A digital retrofit that constitutes a 'substantial modification' may cause the modifier to assume manufacturer obligations."*

**The honest criticism is proportion and sequencing, not absence.** Roughly a third of the deck is governance and two thirds is the regulatory map, and the governance is front-loaded and then not returned to as a spine. **Stated that way it is fair and Pat can act on it. Stated as "nothing applies the definition" it is simply untrue and he will say so.**

## BREAK 3: CAREMARK IS USED CORRECTLY, AND THE REAL POINT IS BETTER

Jürgen: "the room got Caremark, a Delaware doctrine with no force here."

**Caremark appears on slide 37, which is headed "Governance of AI in the USA — Board Accountability", inside a three-slide US section, sourced to In re Caremark (Del. Ch. 1996) and In re McDonald's Corp. (Del. Ch. 2023).** It is in exactly the right place and it is correctly handled.

**But the observation underneath the charge is right, and it is far stronger once corrected.** Look at what each jurisdiction gets:

- **USA:** a precise fiduciary anchor. Caremark, McDonald's, the oversight duty, and the line *"the question a US court will ask is not was the model correct but what system did the board put in place to know."*
- **UK:** SM&CR, Consumer Duty, the Mills Review, and *"using AI does not transfer accountability to the model, vendor or IT function."*
- **Ireland:** **nothing.** Fifteen National Competent Authorities, thirteen Market Surveillance Authorities, nine Article 77 authorities, the AI Office of Ireland, the Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Act 2026, S.I. 366 of 2025. **Not one word about what an Irish director personally owes.**

**So the charge is not that Caremark was misused. It is that Pat gave the American board and the British board a personal liability anchor and gave the Irish board, in Dublin, a list of regulators.** That is a real and serious gap, and it is the single most useful thing in the whole exchange.

# PART TWO — WHAT HOLDS

## HOLD 1: THE IRISH EXAMINER DATE. CONFIRMED, AND IT IS ON THE FACE OF THE DOCUMENT

## HOLD 2: SECTION 228(1)(g) IS ABSENT, AND IT IS THE UPGRADE

Confirmed across all 43 slides. **There is no Irish director duty content anywhere.**

Section 228(1)(g) of the Companies Act 2014 requires a director to exercise the care, skill and diligence of a reasonable person having **both** the knowledge and experience that may reasonably be expected of a person in the same position, **and** the knowledge and experience which the director actually has.

**The second limb is the point, and it is devastating in a room full of directors who have just been briefed.** Everyone who sat through that talk now knows more about AI risk than they did when they walked in, and the standard they are measured against moved with them.

**One correction to Jürgen's version before it is used.** He says Irish law draws no distinction between executive, non-executive, nominee and shadow directors. The **duties** apply identically, and that is right and worth saying. But the objective limb is expressly framed as "a person in the **same position** as the director", so position sits inside the test and what it requires can vary with role. **Any company lawyer will spot the overstatement and use it to discount the rest.** Stated accurately it is still unanswerable: the duty is the same, the "I'm only a NED" escape does not exist, and the subjective limb raises the bar for anyone who has been briefed.

## HOLD 3: ARTICLE 25 IS NOT APPLIED TO THE AI ACT

Confirmed. **Slide 33 raises substantial modification for the Machinery Regulation only**: *"A substantial modification – digital as well as physical – could make the modifier the manufacturer."* Slide 9 asks *"Have you modified an embedded AI process in someone else's products or machinery?"* and *"Have you embedded your own AI process in someone else's products or machinery?"*

**Pat has the concept and applies it in the machinery frame. He never applies it under the AI Act, where a deployer can assume provider obligations by putting its own name on a high-risk system, substantially modifying it, or changing its intended purpose.** White labelling a vendor's tool is ordinary practice among contractors. Good point, fairly made, and it fits naturally onto a slide he has already built.

# PART THREE — MERIDIAN'S OWN FINDINGS

## NEW 1: THE AI LITERACY GAP, AND IT MAY BE BIGGER THAN SECTION 228

**Article 4 AI literacy is the only AI Act obligation that applies to every organisation in that room today, regardless of risk tier, regardless of size, with no threshold.** It has applied since 2 February 2025 and has been enforceable since 2 August 2026.

**It appears once in the entire deck, inside a badge on slide 15**: "Prohibited practices and AI literacy since Feb 2025." It gets no slide, no board question, no construction application.

Meanwhile the deck gives full slides to the EUDI Wallet, the Data Governance Act, the Open Data Directive and the European Health Data Space, none of which bind most of that audience.

**For a construction audience this is the live one.** Every design practice using AI in its software, every contractor running a tool on site, every QS with a model in a spreadsheet has an AI literacy obligation now, and almost none of them know it. **A deck that points at 2 December 2027 while the universal live duty sits in a badge is telling directors they have time.**

**This is a V6 addition, not a criticism of accuracy.** It is also the single most commercially useful gap in the deck.

## NEW 2: THE MACHINERY REGULATION LINE ON SLIDE 15 IS AMBIGUOUS, AND IT ANSWERS JOHN'S QUESTION

John asked what the Omnibus changed in the Machinery Regulation. **Answer: nothing.**

The Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 still applies from **20 January 2027**. What the Digital Omnibus deferred is the **AI Act's** Annex I high-risk obligations for AI embedded in products covered by Annex I legislation, moved to 2 August 2028.

**Slide 15 puts those two facts inside a single Machinery Regulation card**: "Applies from 20 Jan 2027. The Omnibus moved the application date for Annex I to 2 Aug 2028." **Read quickly, that says the Machinery Regulation moved to 2028. It did not.**

**Slides 32 and 33 both get it right**, carrying 20 January 2027 for the make-or-modify audience with no suggestion of deferral. **So slide 15 is the odd one out and it is a wording fix, not an error of substance.** Worth flagging precisely because the make-or-modify audience is the one with the nearest deadline.

## NEW 3: SLIDE 2 CHANGES THE ENTIRE FRAMING QUESTION

**Slide 2 is headed A WARNING and reads:**

> AI systems were used in preparing this presentation.
> AI systems can be **confidently** wrong.
> This presentation is a governance briefing, not legal advice.

**Pat has pre-conceded the point and invited the check, in writing, on his second slide.**

This settles John's third question. **The "his own mistakes prove his thesis" framing does not land as a gotcha, because he asked for it.** Anyone sending him corrections is doing what slide 2 requests. That is as safe a footing as feedback ever gets.

**But it also cuts the other way, and John should know it.** A man who writes that warning and then carries a source dated four days after his own conference has set a standard and missed it. **Which is precisely why the note must be generous, short and useful, rather than thorough.**
