## 1. THE VALUE-BUILDING CASE

**The core principle (well established in SME valuation practice):** a buyer or investor is not really pricing last year's turnover — they are pricing the *reliability and durability* of the income stream they are about to inherit. Two businesses with identical revenue and profit this year can be worth very different amounts depending on how much of that income is contracted and repeatable versus how much has to be re-won, project by project, from scratch.

Why recurring/contracted revenue is worth more, in practical terms:
- **Lower risk to the buyer.** A signed maintenance contract with a renewal date is closer to a known cash flow than a hope that the phone rings with another machine order. Buyers (and their lenders) discount future cash flows for uncertainty — the less certain, the lower the price they will pay for a given level of profit.
- **Less working-capital and pipeline risk.** A reseller's income depends on winning the next deal; a service business's income depends mainly on not losing existing clients — a materially easier thing to underwrite.
- **It signals a business, not a job.** One-off equipment trading is closer to a trading activity that follows the owner's relationships and deal-making than an asset that transfers cleanly to a new owner. A recurring-contract book is an asset (a customer list, a signed pipeline of renewals) that survives a change of hands.
- **It compounds.** Each new maintenance client adds to a growing base rather than replacing last year's one-off sale, so the same sales effort produces a rising, not flat, income line over time.

**General market benchmarks (GENERAL BENCHMARK, not a valuation of Arcade Trader):**
- UK owner-managed SMEs broadly trade in the region of **3x to 5x EBITDA**, with businesses that have strong recurring revenue, clean financial reporting and consistent growth sitting above that range; those reliant on lumpy or one-off income typically sit at or below the bottom of it (SME Business Valuation / ICAEW-aligned commentary, ESTIMATE/GENERAL BENCHMARK, sourced July 2026).
- Businesses with high recurring revenue have been reported to attract multiples roughly **two to three times higher** than comparable businesses reliant on one-off sales (same source, ESTIMATE/GENERAL BENCHMARK).
- Service businesses generally (accountants, agencies, maintenance providers) are valued primarily on earnings/contract quality because the value sits in client relationships and repeat income, not in physical stock — the opposite of how a pure equipment reseller is typically viewed (treated closer to a trading/stock business).

**What this means for Arcade Trader specifically (judgement, not a number):** the pivot Meridian has already recommended — maintenance/service contracts, refurbishment cycles, cashless retrofit, design consultancy — is not just a way to smooth cash flow. It is the single biggest lever John Lynch has to make the business worth more on any future sale, because it converts an asset that is hard to value (a trading relationship and stock) into one that is easy to value (a signed book of contracted, renewing income). No specific multiple should be applied to Arcade Trader today — there is not yet the track record of contracted revenue to support one. The right framing for John Lynch is: build the contract book first, and the valuation conversation gets easier on its own.

## 2. WHAT WOULD MAKE ARCADE TRADER FUNDABLE OR SELLABLE

Whether the audience is a bank/grant assessor lending a small amount now, or a buyer considering an exit years from now, they are largely looking for the same things — and Arcade Trader does not yet have most of them. This is the investment-readiness angle on the "single owner is the binding risk" finding from the growth/economics work: it is not just an operational risk, it is a **valuation and fundability risk**, because both lenders and buyers price against continuity of the business, not the founder.

What a funder or buyer would look for, in rough order of weight:

1. **A recurring, contracted revenue base** — signed maintenance/service agreements with renewal dates, not just a customer list of past buyers. This is the single biggest gap to close and the direct output of the pivot already recommended.
2. **Reduced single-owner dependency.** If John Lynch is the only person who can fix a machine, hold a client relationship, or sign off a quote, the business has no value beyond him — a lender sees repayment risk if he is unavailable; a buyer sees a job they would be buying, not a company. At minimum this means a second trained technician and documented processes (pricing, supplier list, service procedures) that do not live only in John Lynch's head.
3. **A diversified customer base.** Reliance on a handful of one-off project sales (however large, e.g. a single FEC fit-out) concentrates risk. A spread of maintenance clients across seaside arcades, FECs and individual operators is a materially safer book than a few big one-off wins.
4. **Clean, current financial records.** Management accounts, not just year-end filings; clear separation of equipment sales, service revenue, and trade-in/refurbishment income; visibility of margin by revenue line. This is a basic precondition for both a LEO/bank application and any future sale due diligence — and something to establish now rather than reconstruct later.
5. **A real installed base as a documented asset.** The machines Arcade Trader has already sold or serviced across Irish venues are a genuine underlying asset (they are the pool the maintenance/service offer converts into contracts) — but only if there is a proper record of what is installed where, for whom, and under what warranty/service status. At present this appears to sit informally with John Lynch rather than as a structured asset (VERIFICATION FLAG — confirm directly with John Lynch whether an installed-base record exists).

**The honest read:** none of this is unusual or specific to Arcade Trader — it is the standard small-business investment-readiness checklist. What is specific to Arcade Trader is that the strategic pivot already recommended (recurring contracts, Midleton/Killarney leads, cashless retrofit) is the mechanism that builds nearly every item on this list simultaneously. Fundability and the growth strategy are the same project, not two separate ones.

## 3. FUNDING OPTIONS REALISTIC FOR THIS SCALE

This is a small trading/services business at an early pivot stage, not a venture-scale opportunity. The funding routes that fit are correspondingly modest and local. Enterprise Ireland is addressed first precisely to rule it out honestly.

- **Enterprise Ireland — likely not relevant, and should be said plainly.** EI's core supports (High Potential Start-Up funding, scaling supports) target businesses with high-growth, export-oriented trajectories — new-market entry, job creation at scale, and typically a route to significant export revenue. Arcade Trader is a domestic Irish trading/services business serving the Irish amusement sector. Unless the business developed a genuinely exportable product or service (e.g. a cashless-retrofit system sold into UK/EU operators), it sits below EI's usual threshold. This should be stated to John Lynch directly rather than left as a vague possibility — it avoids a wasted application.

- **Local Enterprise Office (LEO) — the most likely first port of call.** Arcade Trader is based in Drogheda, so Louth LEO is the relevant office (VERIFICATION FLAG — confirm Louth LEO specifically, as LEO supports are administered per county, 31 LEOs nationally). Relevant supports:
  - **Feasibility Study Grant** — typically covers up to 50% of eligible costs, commonly in the range of roughly €2,500 to €15,000, for researching market demand and testing viability of a new product/service line (ESTIMATE/GENERAL BENCHMARK, sourced July 2026). Could plausibly fund proper validation of the cashless-retrofit or design-consultancy lines.
  - **Business Priming/Business Expansion Grants** — typically for capital, wage and consultancy costs tied to growth, generally aimed at businesses under 10 employees and turnover under roughly €2 million, which Arcade Trader would fit.
  - LEOs also offer **Trading Online Vouchers** and mentoring, relevant given the digital footprint gap already flagged (arcadetrader.ie as a holding page).
  - LEOs are also the **subsidised access point to Microfinance Ireland** (below) — applying through a LEO gets a lower interest rate than applying direct.

- **Microfinance Ireland (MFI)** — unsecured loans of roughly **€2,000 to €50,000** for microenterprises (fewer than 10 employees, turnover under roughly €2 million), which fits Arcade Trader's scale well. Interest rate roughly 5.5% APR if submitted through a LEO versus roughly 6.5% APR applying direct; terms up to around 5–7 years (VERIFIED via Microfinance Ireland/National Enterprise Hub, sourced July 2026). This is the natural fit for a modest ask such as funding cashless-retrofit stock or a first hire's initial costs.

- **SBCI-backed loans (via participating banks, not SBCI directly)** — the Growth and Sustainability Loan Scheme (GSLS), delivered through banks such as AIB and Bank of Ireland, offers loans from roughly **€25,000 up to €3,000,000**, no security required below roughly €500,000, terms of 7–10 years, eligible for equipment purchases and working capital for growth (VERIFIED via SBCI, sourced July 2026; note the scheme's stated run-until date should be reconfirmed with SBCI/the bank before relying on it, as scheme windows change). This is a plausible route once Arcade Trader has a specific, costed capital need (e.g. stocking cashless-retrofit hardware at scale) and the financial records to support a bank application — better suited to a slightly later stage than the immediate pivot-funding need.

- **Normal bank trade/asset finance** — for financing specific stock or equipment (e.g. cashless card readers, refurbishment parts), ordinary asset finance/hire-purchase from a bank or a specialist lender (e.g. Fexco Asset Finance, an SBCI on-lending partner) is a conventional, lower-friction route once there is a defined asset to finance, rather than a general capital raise.

**Judgement on sequencing:** for John Lynch's likely needs — funding the services pivot, a technician hire, initial cashless-retrofit stock — LEO grant support plus a Microfinance Ireland loan (via the LEO, for the better rate) is the right-sized first move. SBCI/bank routes become relevant later, once there are clean accounts and a specific, larger capital ask (e.g. scaling retrofit stock after Midleton and Killarney convert). Enterprise Ireland should be parked unless an exportable product genuinely emerges.

## 4. THE HONEST BOTTOM LINE

There is no case for external investment in the sense of outside equity, and it should not be positioned as one. This is a small, owner-operated trading business at the very start of a pivot, with no track record yet of the recurring revenue that would make it attractive to any investor beyond a small, cheap loan. Presenting this to John Lynch as an "investment opportunity" would be overselling a stage the business has not reached.

The proportionate sequence is:

1. **Prove the model first, from cash flow.** Convert the Midleton (Coalquay Leisure) and Killarney leads, get the first handful of maintenance contracts signed, and start building the installed-base record. This costs little beyond time and effort already committed in the 100-day plan, and it is the evidence that makes any later funding conversation credible.
2. **Use small, cheap, low-friction funding only where it removes a specific bottleneck** — e.g. a LEO Feasibility Grant to properly cost the cashless-retrofit line, or a Microfinance Ireland loan (via the LEO) if stock or a first hire cannot wait for contract cash flow to build up. These are modest, low-risk instruments appropriate to the scale, not growth capital in the venture sense.
3. **Do not approach a bank for a larger SBCI-backed facility, and do not think about a sale, until the financial records and the contract book exist to support it.** Right now there is not yet a clean revenue-by-line picture, a documented installed base, or contracted recurring income (VERIFICATION FLAG — confirm current bookkeeping/accounting setup with John Lynch; this should be an early, unglamorous priority alongside the sales pivot).
4. **Enterprise Ireland is not a fit at this stage,** and should not be pursued unless the business develops something genuinely exportable.

**Recommendation:** build this organically from the recurring-services pivot already recommended, funded where useful by LEO/Microfinance Ireland support, and revisit the funding and eventual-exit conversation once there is six to twelve months of actual contracted revenue and clean accounts to show. That is also, not coincidentally, exactly what would make the business worth materially more if John Lynch ever wanted to sell it.
