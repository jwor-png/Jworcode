# BUSINESS TRANSFORMATION — ARCADE TRADER

**Prepared by Manager 6 (Business Transformation and Growth), Meridian Intelligence, for John Lynch (via John Webb O'Rourke).** This note is the operational companion to the Strategic Report (commercial/market strategy). It covers HOW the business itself needs to change — process, people, systems, sequencing — not whether to pivot. Private and confidential. 29 July 2026.

---

## 0. THE HEADLINE

The strategy is sound and largely already agreed: pivot from lumpy equipment sales to recurring maintenance and services, anchored on Midleton and Killarney. The risk is not the strategy. The risk is that a one-man business tries to build contracted-service delivery, a second technician, a CRM, a new website, and a sales pipeline into two named accounts, all in the same 100 days, on top of the trading he already does to pay the bills. That is how small transformations stall. This note is about doing the operational build in the right order, at a pace one person and a thin balance sheet can actually sustain.

---

## 1. THE OPERATING MODEL TRANSFORMATION

Selling a maintenance contract is a sales act. Delivering one, month after month, on a stated response time, is an operations act — and it is the operations that will make or break Arcade Trader's credibility with an operator like Coalquay Leisure. What "good" looks like for a small Irish field-service business of this kind:

**Service and dispatch process.** At this scale it does not need to be a helpdesk system — it needs to be *reliable and written down*. A customer (or their machine) reports a fault; the request is logged with a timestamp, not just remembered; John (or a technician) is dispatched with a target response window attached to it; the job is closed out with what was done and what parts were used. JUDGEMENT: even a shared spreadsheet or a low-cost trade tool (e.g. Jobber, Tradify, or a simple Airtable board — all common with UK/Irish sole-trader field-service operators) beats WhatsApp-only dispatch, because WhatsApp has no record, no SLA clock, and no continuity if John is unreachable for a day.

**Spare parts stock management.** Currently, parts holding is presumably informal (whatever is in the van/workshop). A contracted maintenance model needs: a basic list of common wear parts (coin mechs, note validators, buttons, belts, PSUs, cashless readers) with minimum stock levels, and a simple reorder trigger. This does not need software initially — a checklist on a clipboard or spreadsheet, reviewed weekly, is sufficient at 1–2 venues. It becomes necessary infrastructure, not optional polish, once there are multiple contracted sites, because a stockout that breaches a response-time SLA is the fastest way to lose a new-signed contract.

**A basic CRM/customer database.** This is flagged in the brief as apparently absent, and it is the single most important low-cost fix. At minimum: every customer/venue, every machine on site (make, model, serial, install date, warranty status), every service visit, every contract and its renewal date. This can start as a well-structured spreadsheet (one tab per venue, or a simple relational tool like Airtable) — it does not need to be Salesforce. ESTIMATE: building this properly for the current customer base is a 1–2 week task for John or a part-time admin hire, not a software project.

**Technician capacity planning.** With one technician (John), capacity is a hard ceiling: roughly how many venues can realistically be covered on an agreed response time, given driving distances across the relevant counties. JUDGEMENT: before signing more than a handful of contracts, John needs a rough capacity model — venues x average call-outs per year x average hours per call-out + travel, against available hours — so contracts sold do not exceed what one person (plus any subcontractor cover) can actually deliver. This is the direct operational link to the single-owner risk in Section 2.

**SLA definition and tracking.** A maintenance contract needs a stated, honest response time (e.g. "next working day" or "48 hours" rather than an unrealistic same-day promise a one-man band cannot keep), and a simple log of whether each call was met within it. JUDGEMENT: an honest, deliverable SLA (e.g. 48-hour response) sold and consistently met will build more trust with an operator than an aggressive SLA (same-day) that gets missed once. Missed SLAs on a first contract with Coalquay or Killarney would damage the whole services pivot's credibility, not just that one account.

**What "good" looks like overall for a business this size:** not enterprise field-service software, but a disciplined, written, trackable version of what is currently informal — logged jobs, a parts checklist, a customer/machine database, a stated and honoured response time, and a realistic view of how much one technician (plus help) can cover. This is achievable with off-the-shelf low-cost tools and a few days of setup, not a large systems project.

## 2. THE PEOPLE AND CAPABILITY GAP

The single-owner-dependency risk is the binding constraint, already flagged in the Strategic Report, and it is worth being blunt about why: a contracted maintenance business is selling *reliability*, and reliability that depends entirely on one person's health, holidays, and hours is not really reliability. An operator signing a multi-year maintenance contract is implicitly asking "what happens when John is sick, or on a family holiday, or simply overloaded because he took on three new venues at once?" Today there is no good answer. That has to change before the business scales past a small handful of contracted venues, though it does not have to change before the first one or two.

**Realistic path — sequenced by cost and risk, not by ambition:**

1. **Subcontractor/partner arrangement first (lowest cost, fastest, most reversible).** JUDGEMENT: before hiring anyone, the more realistic near-term move is an informal or semi-formal arrangement with another trades-qualified technician (electrician, vending/gaming-machine engineer, or similar) who can be called on a day-rate basis for cover — holiday cover, overflow, or a second pair of hands on a big job. This gives depth without payroll commitment or fixed cost, and is common practice among small Irish field-service operators before they hire directly. It also serves as a low-risk trial of a future hire.

2. **Part-time or fixed-hours technician/installer once there is a proven, contracted revenue base to support it (not before).** ESTIMATE: a general operative/apprentice-level technician in Ireland in 2026 is realistically in the region of EUR 32,000–40,000 gross annually for a full-time role, before employer PRSI (c.11.15%) and other on-costs, which typically add roughly 12–15% on top — so all-in cost is likely in the region of EUR 36,000–46,000 for a full-time hire (ESTIMATE, not sourced to a specific 2026 pay survey; John should sanity-check against actual local trade rates). A part-time arrangement (2–3 days/week) would scale down proportionately. JUDGEMENT: given the per-venue economics modelled in the Strategic Report (c. EUR 15,000–45,000/venue/year, ESTIMATE), a full-time second technician is only supportable once there are roughly 3–5 contracted venues generating recurring revenue — do not hire ahead of that unless a subcontractor/partner model is proving genuinely insufficient.

3. **What the hire needs to look like.** Not necessarily a specialist arcade engineer — those are scarce and expensive. JUDGEMENT: a general electro-mechanical aptitude (vending machine engineer, coin-op/ATM technician, general electrician with some electronics) trained up on the specific equipment is a realistic and much cheaper route than trying to recruit a like-for-like Arcade Trader specialist. Training time should be budgeted (weeks, not months, for common fault types) and John's own knowledge needs to be captured in simple written form (a basic fault/repair guide per common machine type) so it is not solely in his head — this is a cheap, high-value piece of work John can do himself over a few evenings, and it directly reduces key-person risk even before any hire is made.

4. **Do not over-structure early.** JUDGEMENT: forming a formal company structure change, a second director, or equity-sharing arrangement is not needed at this stage and would be premature — the near-term fix is operational depth (a subcontractor and eventually a part-time hire), not corporate restructuring. Revisit structure only once there is a genuine second full-time person and the business needs to formalise around them.

## 3. BRAND, DIGITAL AND SALES INFRASTRUCTURE

Keep this proportionate — a small owner-operated business needs functional infrastructure, not a rebrand.

**Website.** The holding page needs to become a simple, credible site: what Arcade Trader does (equipment, install, maintenance, refurb, cashless, design consultancy), a short "About" establishing credibility (years trading, European experience), and — critically — case studies or before/after examples of real work, even 2–3 to start. ESTIMATE: a straightforward small-business website (5–8 pages, no e-commerce) built on a template platform (Squarespace, Wix, or WordPress) is realistically EUR 1,500–4,000 if outsourced to a local freelancer/small agency, or largely free (bar a template/hosting cost of roughly EUR 15–30/month) if John or a capable contact builds it directly on a template. Timeframe: 2–4 weeks for a first working version, not months.

**CRM/pipeline tool.** As above (Section 1) — this doubles as both an operational tool (service/customer database) and a sales tool (tracking the Coalquay, Killarney and other named leads through a pipeline: contacted, proposal sent, follow-up due, won/lost). A tool like Airtable, HubSpot's free tier, or even a well-organised Google Sheet is sufficient at this scale. ESTIMATE: EUR 0–50/month depending on tool chosen; a day or two to set up properly.

**Invoicing/quoting system.** If not already using one, a simple cloud accounting/invoicing tool (e.g. QuickBooks, Sage, or a lighter tool like Invoice Ninja) that can generate professional quotes and track what is owed. ESTIMATE: EUR 20–40/month for a small-business plan. This matters more than it sounds for the services pivot specifically, because contracted maintenance needs recurring/scheduled invoicing (annual or quarterly billing), which a lot of basic tools handle automatically once set up — removing a manual admin burden from John.

**Total proportionate first-infrastructure spend:** ESTIMATE, in the region of EUR 2,000–5,000 one-off plus roughly EUR 50–120/month ongoing — a modest, affordable outlay for a business this size, not a transformation budget. This should not be a blocker to starting.

## 4. CHANGE SEQUENCING — OPERATIONAL BUILD ORDER

The Strategic Report's 100-day plan is commercially sequenced (pipeline, website, offer definition, approach Coalquay/Killarney). This section adds the operational precondition layer underneath it — what must exist internally before John can credibly sign a first contract, versus what can run in parallel or come after.

**Must exist BEFORE signing the first maintenance contract (non-negotiable — this is what makes the promise deliverable, not just sellable):**
- A defined, honest SLA (response time) that matches what one technician (plus any subcontractor cover) can actually deliver — i.e., the capacity check in Section 1 must be done first.
- A minimal customer/machine database covering at least the venue(s) being contracted — cannot manage a maintenance obligation without knowing what equipment is on site.
- A basic logged-job process (even a spreadsheet) so a contracted response time can be tracked and, if challenged, evidenced.
- A parts checklist for the machine types at that specific venue, with stock or a fast supply line confirmed.

**Can be built in parallel with the first contract approach (does not need to be finished first):**
- The new website — helpful for credibility with Coalquay/Killarney but not a hard blocker to a warm, relationship-led approach to an operator who already knows the sector.
- The formal CRM/pipeline tool, beyond the minimal spreadsheet above — can be built out properly while the first proposal is in motion.
- Wider customer database beyond the immediate target venues.

**Comes AFTER first revenue (sequenced later, not because it doesn't matter, but because it needs proof of demand and cash flow to justify the cost):**
- The subcontractor/partner arrangement can be explored early (low cost, low commitment) but a paid part-time or full-time technician hire should wait until there are 3–5 contracted venues, per Section 2.
- Full invoicing/quoting system upgrade, if not already in place — nice to have early, not a blocker.
- Any wider service-line expansion (design consultancy formalisation, managed/revenue-share placement) — explicitly held for later in the Strategic Report already.

**JUDGEMENT — the one hard rule:** do not let the Midleton or Killarney approach get ahead of the capacity and SLA-honesty check. It is better to approach Coalquay Leisure in month 2 with a deliverable, honest offer than to approach them in month 1 with an aggressive offer that cannot be met. The commercial 100-day plan and this operational sequencing should be read together, not the commercial one alone.

## 5. RISK OF THE TRANSFORMATION ITSELF — LOAD ON JOHN LYNCH

This is the honest part. In year one, John is being asked to simultaneously: keep the existing trading business running (the thing that currently pays the bills), build a CRM and database from scratch, define and stand behind a new SLA-backed product, oversee a new website build, pursue two named high-value leads (Midleton, Killarney) that each require proposal work and relationship management, and — if it progresses — recruit, train and manage a first subcontractor or hire. That is a lot for one person, and it is the realistic way small owner-operator transformations fail: not because the strategy was wrong, but because the owner is stretched thin enough that both the new initiative and the existing trading business suffer at once.

**How to sequence it so it does not stall the existing business:**

- **Timebox the admin build.** The CRM/database and parts checklist (Section 1) should be done in short, bounded bursts (a few hours a week, or one concentrated week) rather than being an open-ended background task competing with client work indefinitely.
- **Do not chase both Midleton and Killarney at full intensity simultaneously in month one.** JUDGEMENT: pick the warmer, more time-sensitive one first (Midleton/Coalquay, given the fit-out timing already flagged as time-limited in the Strategic Report) and let Killarney follow once the first proposal is out the door. Running two full new-business pursuits at once, on top of ordinary trading, on top of building the CRM, is the overload scenario to avoid.
- **Get outside help for the non-core build tasks.** The website build and initial CRM/tool setup are exactly the kind of one-off, bounded, outsourceable tasks (a freelancer or capable contact) that should not consume John's own time, given his time is better spent on the technical/relationship work only he can do (the actual machine servicing, and the Coalquay/Killarney relationships).
- **Protect existing trading revenue explicitly.** The transformation should be planned as additive capacity, not a switch of John's existing time away from paying trade work in month one. The 100-day plan already sequences "launch offer" before "win work" — the operational read is: do not let new-business pursuit or admin-building crowd out the trading income that is currently funding the business through this transition.
- **Revisit realistically at day 100.** JUDGEMENT: if, at the 100-day mark, John is significantly behind on the operational build (Sections 1–3) because trading work has (rightly) taken priority, that is a signal to slow the commercial pursuit of new venues, not a signal to push harder on both fronts. A stalled, under-resourced first contract with Coalquay would do more reputational damage to the whole pivot than a slower, well-delivered start.

**Bottom line:** the transformation is right-sized for this business if it is sequenced — capacity and SLA honesty before signing, minimal viable systems before scale, subcontractor before hire, one lead pursued properly before the second — and wrong-sized if John tries to do all five sections of this note in the first 100 days at once. Pace this over 6–12 months, not 100 days, even though the commercial 100-day plan for first contact and proposal work is realistic on its own terms.
