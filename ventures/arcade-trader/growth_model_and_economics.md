## 1. THE CORE STRATEGIC MOVE

**The move: reposition from equipment reseller to recurring-revenue services company, with equipment sales as the front door rather than the whole business.**

### Why this is the right central bet

The top risk in the draft briefing is correct and it is the one that matters: dependence on project / one-off sales. That single dependency creates three compounding problems.

1. **Revenue is lumpy and unpredictable.** A reseller starts every quarter at zero and must win it again. Cash flow swings with a handful of large orders. One slow quarter, or one delayed FEC (family entertainment centre) fit-out, and the year is in trouble.
2. **The customer relationship ends at delivery.** The moment the machines are installed, the commercial relationship goes cold until the customer happens to need more equipment. That is the most expensive possible way to run a customer base: pay full acquisition cost every time, capture value once.
3. **The business is a margin-taker, not a margin-setter.** As a reseller between European manufacturers and Irish venues, Arcade Trader's pricing power is capped by what is essentially a distribution spread, and that spread is exactly what a UK or European supplier can undercut on any given deal.

Services fix all three at once. A maintenance contract turns a one-time buyer into an annuity. Refurbishment turns installed equipment into a recurring revenue event every few years. Trade-in turns the customer's next upgrade into two transactions for Arcade Trader (the used machine in, the new machine out) instead of a lost sale to a competitor. The installed base becomes the asset, not each individual sale.

### The real insight: Arcade Trader already sits on the asset it is not monetising

Every machine Arcade Trader has ever sold into an Irish or European venue is a service opportunity that is currently earning nothing after the invoice is paid. The engineering capability named as a strength is precisely the capability needed to run maintenance, refurbishment and spares. This is not a pivot into an unrelated business. It is charging for the after-life of the equipment the business already understands better than anyone else in the room. **Judgement: the pivot is low-conceptual-risk because it monetises existing competence and an existing installed base, rather than requiring new capability.**

### What it does to valuation and resilience

- **Resilience.** Recurring contract revenue provides a floor. If maintenance contracts cover the fixed cost base (owner drawings, van, workshop, insurance, one or two engineers), then project sales become upside rather than survival. That is the difference between a business that is anxious every quarter and one that can plan.
- **Valuation.** This is the strongest financial argument. A one-off-sales distribution business is typically valued on a low multiple of profit, because a buyer is buying goodwill and a supplier relationship that may not persist. A business with a book of contracted, renewing maintenance revenue is valued on a materially higher multiple, because the buyer is buying a predictable annuity with visible retention. **Judgement: for an owner with succession / eventual-exit in mind (single owner, see section 5), converting even 30 to 40 per cent of revenue to contracted recurring income is likely the single biggest lever on the ultimate sale value of the business.** The recurring pound is worth more than the project pound both while you own it and when you sell it (economics in section 3).

### What "Ireland's leading specialist amusement industry services company" actually requires

Not scale. Positioning and proof. Specifically:

- **A named, contracted book of service clients** across arcades, FECs, seaside / leisure operators, holiday parks and hospitality venues with amusements. The claim is credible the day there is a visible roster, not before.
- **Guaranteed response.** "Leading services company" in an equipment context means an SLA (service level agreement): a promised response time for breakdowns. A dead machine is lost coin-drop revenue every hour it is down, so speed of response is the product. This is the thing UK / European suppliers structurally cannot match in Ireland: they are not on the island.
- **Full-lifecycle coverage.** Supply, install, service, refurbish, trade-in, dispose / replace. Being the one call the operator makes for the whole equipment lifecycle is the moat.
- **A visible digital and reference presence** so that when an operator searches, Arcade Trader is the obvious Irish specialist (addresses the digital-profile weakness directly).

The realistic prize is to own the Irish island market as the default services partner, using local presence and response time as the defensible advantage against larger but distant competitors. **Judgement: leadership here is a positioning and reliability claim, not a size claim, and is achievable for an SME.**

## 2. THE OPERATING MODEL

Seven revenue lines. They are not equal and they should not launch at once. They form a lifecycle, and each one feeds the next.

| Line | Type | Role | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Equipment supply | One-off | Front door and installed-base builder | Keep - the base |
| (b) Annual maintenance / service contracts | Recurring | The strategic core | Priority 1 |
| (g) Spare parts | Recurring / repeat | Attaches to maintenance, high margin | Priority 1 (with b) |
| (c) Refurbishment cycles | Repeat, cyclical | Mid-life value capture | Priority 2 |
| (d) Trade-in and used-equipment resale | Transactional, margin-rich | Defends the next new sale, opens value stock | Priority 2 |
| (f) Consultancy / design (arcades, FECs) | Project, high-margin | Wins the whole fit-out and the service contract with it | Priority 3 |
| (e) Managed / revenue-share with venues | Recurring, capital-heavier | The long game, highest value, highest risk | Priority 4 (selective) |

### How they fit together (the lifecycle logic)

A venue is designed (f) → equipped (a) → maintained under contract (b) with parts (g) → refurbished at mid-life (c) → old units taken in trade (d) and resold as value stock (d), while the venue re-equips (a) → and for the right partner, run on revenue-share (e). **The whole point is that one customer relationship now generates six or seven billing events over the equipment lifecycle instead of one.**

### Sequencing and reasoning

**Priority 1 - Maintenance contracts + spares (launch first, immediately).**
This is the thesis. It requires no new capability and no capital, only a productised offer, a price and a contract. Start by converting the existing installed base: every past customer is a warm target for a service agreement. Spares attach naturally - a machine under contract needs its parts from Arcade Trader, not a grey import. Launch these together.

**Priority 2 - Refurbishment and trade-in (fast follow, months 3 to 9).**
Refurbishment is the natural upsell to a maintenance client whose equipment is ageing and the honest alternative to "buy new". Trade-in is the commercial defence mechanism: it removes the customer's reason to shop elsewhere for their upgrade and it builds a stock of used machines that can be refurbished and resold at strong margin, or placed into revenue-share sites. These two are joined at the hip.

**Priority 3 - Consultancy / design (opportunistic from day one, formalised later).**
High margin and high influence: whoever designs the arcade specifies the equipment and, ideally, signs the service contract on day one. Do not build a consultancy practice; use design as the wedge that captures the whole lifecycle on new-build and refit projects (this is where the Midleton / Midleton-type opportunities sit).

**Priority 4 - Managed / revenue-share (selective, later, disciplined).**
Highest lifetime value because Arcade Trader shares the coin-drop, but it ties up capital in machines Arcade Trader continues to own and exposes the business to a site's footfall risk. **Judgement: only pursue revenue-share on proven sites with trusted operators, funded partly by refurbished trade-in stock so the capital at risk is depreciated stock rather than new cash. Do not lead with this.**

## 3. ILLUSTRATIVE UNIT ECONOMICS (ESTIMATE - clearly flagged)

### Benchmark context (VERIFIED / ESTIMATE)

- Amusement / gaming machine service and maintenance in the Ireland/UK market is commonly sold either as an annual contract per machine or as a per-site contract with an SLA, frequently with a call-out / parts structure on top. Public list pricing is thin because deals are negotiated, so precise figures below are ESTIMATE. (Judgement based on general equipment-service market structure; to be validated against John's own quoting history, which is the best available benchmark.)
- General cross-industry rule of thumb (VERIFIED as a widely used planning heuristic): an annual maintenance / service contract on installed equipment is often priced at roughly 8 to 15 per cent of the equipment's capital value per year. Applied to amusement equipment this is a reasonable starting frame.

### Illustrative maintenance-contract model (per machine, per year)

Assume a typical arcade / redemption machine with a capital value of EUR 8,000 to EUR 15,000 (ESTIMATE; wide range by machine type).

| Item | Illustrative figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Annual maintenance contract value per machine | EUR 900 to EUR 1,600 | ~10 to 12 per cent of capital value, ESTIMATE |
| Direct cost to serve per machine per year (engineer time, travel, consumables) | EUR 350 to EUR 650 | ESTIMATE |
| Gross margin per machine per year | ~55 to 65 per cent | Modelled |
| Spare parts revenue (attached, on top) | EUR 150 to EUR 500 | ESTIMATE, high margin |

**Per-venue view.** A modest arcade or FEC with 15 to 30 machines under contract implies roughly **EUR 15,000 to EUR 45,000 of contracted annual revenue per venue** (ESTIMATE), before parts and refurbishment. Sold per site with a single SLA rather than per machine, this is simpler to administer and stickier.

### Why recurring compounds and project revenue does not

The mechanism, in plain numbers (illustrative).

- **Project model:** Arcade Trader sells EUR 200,000 of equipment in Year 1 at, say, a 20 per cent gross margin = EUR 40,000 gross. In Year 2, the business starts again at zero and must find another EUR 200,000 of orders to stand still.
- **Recurring model:** Arcade Trader signs 10 venues at EUR 25,000 annual contract each = EUR 250,000 recurring at ~60 per cent margin = EUR 150,000 gross. Assume 90 per cent retention and modest net new adds. Year 2 does not start at zero; it starts at ~EUR 225,000 retained and builds from there.

```
Contracted recurring revenue, illustrative build (90% retention, +8 venues/yr)
Year 1:  10 venues   ~EUR 250k
Year 2:  17 venues   ~EUR 425k   (9 retained + 8 new)
Year 3:  23 venues   ~EUR 575k
```

The project line saws up and down around a flat average. The recurring line ratchets upward and only falls if customers actively leave. **That is the whole argument: recurring revenue accumulates a base, project revenue resets it.**

### Why the recurring pound is worth more than the project pound

Three reasons, all real:

1. **Predictability.** Contracted, renewing revenue can be planned, financed and staffed against. A EUR 100k recurring book lets you hire; EUR 100k of hoped-for projects does not.
2. **Margin quality and stickiness.** Service margins here are modelled higher than distribution margins (60 per cent vs 20 per cent illustrative), and a customer under an SLA with their equipment history held by Arcade Trader does not casually switch.
3. **Valuation multiple.** As in section 1, a buyer pays a higher multiple for contracted recurring income than for last year's project sheet. The same EUR 1 of profit is worth more when it recurs. **Judgement: this is the single most important economic reason to make the pivot, over and above the cash-flow smoothing.**

## 5. RISKS, SUCCESS FACTORS AND THE HONEST CHALLENGE

### The honest challenge

The strategy is sound. The binding constraint is **John**. Every strength in the draft (industry knowledge, engineering, European relationships) and every action in the 100-day plan currently sits on one person. **A services business sells a promise of response, and a promise of response that depends on a single individual is fragile the first week that individual is ill, away or overloaded.** This is the thing that most threatens the whole thesis, and it must be named to the board plainly.

### Key risks

1. **Key-person / succession risk (single owner).** The most serious. It caps growth (you can only sign as many SLAs as one or two people can honour), it is the reason a buyer would discount the business, and it puts continuity at risk. Mitigation: the second-engineer hire is not a "nice to have", it is strategic, and documenting the service process so it is not all in John's head.
2. **Cash flow through the transition.** Building a recurring book takes time to compound (section 3), while project revenue may dip as attention shifts. The transition can be the leanest period. Mitigation: keep selling equipment throughout (do not abandon the base to chase the new model), launch contracts to the warm installed base first for fast wins, and stage the capital-heavy revenue-share model to last.
3. **Digital-profile gap.** In a market where operators increasingly search before they call, near-invisibility online means Arcade Trader is not in the consideration set. Mitigation: the website + case studies is a top-three action, not cosmetic.
4. **Competition from UK / European suppliers.** They may beat Arcade Trader on unit price on any given machine. Mitigation: do not fight on the machine price, fight on the thing they cannot deliver from another country - local presence, fast on-site response, and full-lifecycle ownership of the equipment. Turn their scale into their weakness (distant, slow, transactional).

### The two or three things that must go right

1. **The installed base converts to contracts.** If past customers sign maintenance agreements, the model is proven and the recurring base starts compounding. This is the make-or-break, and it is the most controllable.
2. **Arcade Trader stops being one person deep.** A second delivery capability (engineer) has to arrive early enough to let the contract book grow without breaking the SLA promise.
3. **The SLA is actually honoured.** The entire premium positioning rests on response time. Keep the promise and the reputation compounds; break it and "leading services company" becomes a liability. Reliability is the product.

## 6. AI ANGLE (light and realistic)

The draft's "AI-assisted customer support and internal operations" is right to be modest. For a business this size, AI is a quiet efficiency layer that lets one or two people run a larger service book. It is not a strategy and should not be sold as one. Where it genuinely helps, ranked by real payback:

- **Scheduling and dispatch (highest real value).** Once there are dozens of machines under SLA across the island, routing engineers efficiently and tracking response times is exactly where lightweight automation earns its keep. This directly protects the SLA promise that the whole model rests on.
- **Quoting.** Assisted drafting of quotes and service proposals from a template and a parts list saves John hours and speeds response to enquiries. Genuinely useful, low risk.
- **Parts inventory.** Predicting and flagging spares to reorder based on the installed base and service history avoids the dead-machine-waiting-for-a-part problem, which is a direct SLA and revenue risk. Useful once volume justifies it.
- **Customer support.** An assistant to handle routine enquiries, booking requests and first-line questions so John is not the switchboard. Helpful, but keep a human on anything that is a live breakdown - a down machine is losing the customer money and wants a person.

**Honest framing for the board:** AI here is plumbing, not a product. It makes a small team behave like a bigger one and helps keep the response-time promise as the contract book grows. That is worth doing, incrementally, from the point where contract volume creates real admin load. It is not a headline, and it should not distract from the actual strategic work, which is signing recurring contracts. **Judgement: adopt AI as an operational efficiency once there are enough contracts to administer, not before, and never let it touch the live-breakdown response, which is the human moment the whole brand is built on.**

## BOTTOM LINE

The pivot from reseller to recurring-revenue services company is the right central move, and it is lower-risk than it looks because it monetises capability and an installed base Arcade Trader already has. Lead with maintenance contracts to the existing customer base, attach spares, fast-follow with refurbishment and trade-in, use design as the wedge on new fit-outs, and hold revenue-share for later and for proven sites. The economics favour the recurring pound over the project pound on cash-flow stability, margin and, above all, valuation. The two things that must go right: the installed base signs contracts, and the business stops being one person deep before the SLA promise gets stretched.
